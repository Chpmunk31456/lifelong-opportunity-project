## What peer support is

Peer support is generally grounded in shared lived experience and recovery-oriented, person-centered relationships. It can be used in behavioral-health, recovery, community, school, workplace, and other support settings. A Peer Support Specialist is **not automatically a therapist, psychologist, social worker, nurse, or other licensed clinician**. Scope, credentialing, supervision, reimbursement, and employer requirements vary by jurisdiction and program.

Lived experience may be central to a peer role, but job seekers should not feel pressured to disclose diagnoses, treatment history, disability status, substance-use history, or other sensitive personal information beyond what a lawful credentialing or employment process actually requires.

## Current pathway guidance

### United States

Use the [SAMHSA Core Competencies for Peer Workers](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/recovery/peer-support-workers/core-competencies) as a practice and workforce-development framework, not as a universal national license. State, Medicaid, payer, program, and employer requirements can differ.

For training and funding, search current state behavioral-health authorities, American Job Centers, eligible-training-provider lists, community colleges, and [Apprenticeship.gov](https://www.apprenticeship.gov/) using terms such as **peer support**, **behavioral health**, **recovery support**, and **community health worker**. Verify that a program is active and applicable to your state before relying on it.

Ask prospective employers whether required peer-support training, examination fees, continuing education, supervision, onboarding, or credential-renewal costs are employer-paid or reimbursable.

### Canada

Canada's [Job Bank](https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/summary-occupation/24973/ca) maps **Peer Support Worker** to the broader NOC 42201 Social and community service workers group. Requirements and prospects vary by province, territory, employer, and program. The [Mental Health Commission of Canada peer-support guidance](https://mentalhealthcommission.ca/resource/guidelines-for-the-practice-and-training-of-peer-support/) is useful for practice principles, but its training guidelines were published in 2013 and should not be presented as a current universal Canadian credential standard.

Check employers, colleges, provincial or territorial workforce programs, community mental-health organizations, and current grant-funded initiatives for training support. Historic pilots or grants should not be assumed to remain open.

### Colombia

Colombia has established community mental-health and psychosocial-support pathways through the [Ministerio de Salud y Protección Social](https://www.minsalud.gov.co/salud/publica/salud-mental/Paginas/salud-mental-comunitaria.aspx), including community-oriented settings and territorial mental-health resources. These pathways should **not** be relabeled as a U.S.-style national Peer Support Specialist certification unless a Colombian authority establishes such a credential.

[SENA Betowa](https://betowa.sena.edu.co/oferta?search=salud+mental) may list free or low-cost adjacent mental-health training. These offerings can support learning but are not evidence of a national Peer Support Specialist credential. Availability, prerequisites, modality, and enrollment dates can change.

### Latin America

Across Latin America, titles, formal recognition, funding mechanisms, and community mental-health structures differ by country. Verify the target country's official health, education/training, labor, and employment portals. Distinguish volunteer peer-support activity from paid employment, and do not treat a short course certificate as a regulated professional credential unless the relevant authority says it is one.

## Practical funding and training checklist

Before paying for training or a credential:

1. Verify the exact credential or training required by employers in your target location.
2. Check whether the issuing organization is recognized by the relevant state, province, territory, payer, program, or employer.
3. Search government workforce programs, public training providers, community colleges, nonprofit mental-health organizations, and active apprenticeships for free or subsidized options.
4. Ask employers about paid training, tuition assistance, examination reimbursement, continuing education, supervision, and credential-renewal support.
5. Compare scholarships or grants only after confirming current eligibility, application dates, covered costs, and whether repayment is required.
6. Avoid programs that promise guaranteed employment, guaranteed certification portability, or unusually high income without verifiable evidence.

## Scope and safety

This guide supports practical career exploration, accessible learning, ethical use of artificial intelligence, privacy and safety, realistic education pathways, portfolio development, job-search preparation, and verification through current official sources.

Peer workers should follow employer and jurisdictional procedures for emergencies, safeguarding, mandated reporting, and escalation. Peer support complements appropriate clinical and emergency services; it does not replace them.

## Important notice

This material provides general educational and career-planning information. It does not guarantee employment, income, admission, funding, licensing, certification, promotion, reimbursement, or any other result. Requirements and opportunities vary by jurisdiction, employer, payer, program, and time. Verify important decisions through current official sources and qualified professionals.
