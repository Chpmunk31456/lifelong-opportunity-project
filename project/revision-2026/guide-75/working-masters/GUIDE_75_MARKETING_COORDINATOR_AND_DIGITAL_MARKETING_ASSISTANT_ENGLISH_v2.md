## What this career is

Marketing coordinators and digital marketing assistants help organizations plan, prepare, publish, track, and improve marketing activity. Depending on the employer, the work may include campaign calendars, social media, email, website content, customer relationship management systems, ecommerce support, digital advertising support, search-engine optimization basics, analytics, event promotion, vendor coordination, and reporting.

Titles vary widely. Employers may use marketing assistant, digital marketing assistant, marketing coordinator, campaign coordinator, content coordinator, social media coordinator, ecommerce marketing assistant, CRM coordinator, communications assistant, growth marketing assistant, or marketing specialist. A title alone does not establish seniority or authority, so always read the actual job description.

This guide uses **O*NET-SOC 13-1161.00 — Market Research Analysts and Marketing Specialists** as a broad U.S. benchmark. That occupation is generally more analytical and professional than some entry-level assistant jobs. The guide therefore separates junior support work from specialist, analyst, and manager responsibilities instead of pretending every title maps perfectly to one occupation code.

## Why this role can be an opportunity

Marketing work can combine writing, organization, customer understanding, design coordination, data interpretation, technology, teamwork, and business awareness. Skills can transfer from customer service, sales, retail, hospitality, office administration, ecommerce, nonprofit work, social media, communications, graphic production, or event support.

This field may suit people who enjoy:

- organizing campaigns and deadlines;
- writing or editing clear messages;
- working with spreadsheets and digital tools;
- learning what audiences respond to;
- checking details before publication;
- coordinating with designers, vendors, sales teams, and managers;
- measuring results and improving future work;
- balancing creativity with evidence and business goals.

The work can also be deadline-driven. Campaign launches, promotions, product releases, executive requests, customer complaints, platform changes, and technical problems can create pressure. A short course, supervised project, internship, volunteer assignment, or junior marketing role can provide a realistic preview before paying for expensive training.

## Assistant, coordinator, specialist, analyst, and manager are different responsibility levels

### Marketing or digital marketing assistant

An assistant may:

- update campaign calendars;
- prepare draft social posts or emails;
- resize or organize approved creative assets;
- enter campaign data into approved systems;
- check links and formatting;
- maintain contact or content lists;
- schedule approved content;
- collect performance metrics;
- prepare first-pass reports;
- document action items;
- escalate errors, complaints, or approval questions.

### Marketing coordinator

A coordinator may additionally:

- coordinate campaign timelines and owners;
- manage approved content schedules;
- work with vendors or agencies;
- coordinate email, social, web, event, or ecommerce activity;
- track budget against authorized amounts;
- maintain CRM or marketing-automation workflows within assigned permissions;
- coordinate review and approval steps;
- monitor campaign performance;
- prepare dashboards or summaries;
- support launches and cross-functional meetings.

### Specialist or analyst

A specialist or analyst may have deeper responsibility for:

- audience or market analysis;
- campaign optimization;
- paid media;
- SEO/SEM;
- CRM segmentation;
- analytics and attribution;
- experimentation;
- marketing automation;
- competitive research;
- forecasting or performance interpretation.

### Manager

A manager may own broader decisions involving:

- strategy;
- budget approval;
- staffing;
- agency selection;
- vendor contracts;
- brand governance;
- compliance review coordination;
- campaign approval;
- pricing or promotion decisions;
- executive reporting;
- escalation and risk acceptance.

These boundaries are examples only. Employer policy, contracts, local law, platform rules, and delegated authority determine what a worker may actually do.

## Typical responsibilities

Depending on the role, a marketing coordinator or digital marketing assistant may:

- maintain a marketing calendar and campaign milestones;
- collect campaign requirements from managers or internal clients;
- draft routine content for review;
- coordinate approved images, video, copy, landing pages, and emails;
- prepare social-media schedules;
- update website or ecommerce content through approved systems;
- support email marketing and CRM workflows;
- organize lead lists or customer segments under approved rules;
- coordinate webinars, events, product launches, or promotions;
- maintain UTM naming or campaign-tracking conventions where assigned;
- support SEO basics such as metadata, descriptive headings, internal links, and content organization;
- gather analytics from approved sources;
- prepare recurring performance reports;
- compare results against campaign goals;
- check links, spelling, dates, prices, disclaimers, and approved claims before publication;
- coordinate vendor invoices or purchase requests within delegated authority;
- organize digital assets and version history;
- document approvals and campaign changes;
- support accessibility checks for digital content;
- follow privacy, consent, suppression, and preference-management processes;
- report suspected phishing, account compromise, fraudulent requests, or data exposure;
- archive campaign records according to policy.

## Professional boundaries

A marketing title does **not** automatically authorize a worker to:

- make unsupported health, financial, legal, safety, environmental, or product-performance claims;
- promise a price, discount, refund, warranty, availability, or service term that has not been approved;
- buy advertising or commit funds without delegated authority;
- change vendor payment or bank instructions without independent verification and approval;
- upload customer lists, credentials, payment information, contracts, or confidential business data into an unapproved AI or cloud tool;
- bypass consent, unsubscribe, suppression, or preference controls;
- scrape, purchase, combine, or reuse personal data in ways the organization has not approved;
- misrepresent customer reviews, testimonials, endorsements, statistics, or research;
- impersonate customers, employees, competitors, influencers, or public officials;
- publish confidential launch plans or unreleased information;
- claim a website, campaign, or document is legally compliant or accessibility-certified without qualified review;
- treat AI output as the final authority for regulated claims, prices, statistics, translations, accessibility commitments, privacy requirements, targeting logic, or legal obligations.

When authority is unclear, pause the publication or transaction and escalate to the appropriate manager, legal/compliance function, privacy team, cybersecurity team, finance function, accessibility contact, brand owner, or platform administrator.

## A practical campaign workflow

### 1. Define

Clarify:

- campaign objective;
- target audience;
- approved offer or message;
- channels;
- budget authority;
- campaign owner;
- required approvals;
- success metrics;
- privacy or accessibility requirements;
- launch date and dependencies.

### 2. Prepare

Build or coordinate:

- copy;
- creative assets;
- landing pages;
- email or social schedules;
- tracking links;
- audience or segment rules;
- test cases;
- review checklist;
- escalation contacts.

### 3. Verify

Before launch, confirm:

- names, dates, links, prices, and approved claims;
- audience and suppression rules;
- permissions and account access;
- mobile and desktop formatting where relevant;
- meaningful alt text or other accessibility requirements;
- tracking tags and campaign naming;
- approval status;
- budget or spending limits;
- legal/compliance disclaimers where required by the organization.

### 4. Publish or launch

Use only approved systems and delegated permissions. Record significant changes. If a platform, account, audience, or spend setting looks wrong, stop and escalate rather than improvising.

### 5. Monitor and close

After launch:

- monitor for errors and complaints;
- capture metrics from authoritative systems;
- compare results with campaign objectives;
- document lessons learned;
- reconcile open tasks and invoices;
- archive records according to policy;
- remove unnecessary access where that is part of the assigned role.

## Privacy, consent, and customer data

Marketing can involve names, email addresses, telephone numbers, purchase history, browsing behaviour, preferences, location information, customer identifiers, audience segments, and campaign responses. The rules governing these data vary by jurisdiction, employer, platform, and use case.

Practical habits include:

- collect only the data needed for an approved purpose;
- use employer-approved systems;
- follow consent, unsubscribe, suppression, and preference-management workflows;
- do not export customer lists to personal devices or personal email;
- restrict access to people who need it;
- verify recipients before sharing reports or lists;
- protect credentials and API keys;
- follow retention and deletion rules;
- escalate requests to reuse data for a new purpose if authorization is unclear;
- do not assume that information visible online is free to collect or reuse.

This guide provides operational awareness, not jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

## Cybersecurity and fraud prevention

Marketing teams are attractive targets because they may control public websites, social-media accounts, advertising budgets, mailing lists, domains, and vendor relationships.

Good practices include:

- enable multifactor authentication where supported;
- use unique passwords and an approved password manager;
- verify unexpected requests to transfer account ownership, add administrators, change domains, or alter vendor payment details;
- confirm unusual invoice or ad-spend requests through an independent approved contact method;
- report phishing quickly;
- avoid sharing passwords in chat or email;
- use least-privilege access;
- remove access when staff or vendors leave when that is your responsibility;
- protect recovery codes and administrator accounts;
- keep software and browsers updated according to organizational policy.

## Accessibility and inclusive digital marketing

Accessible marketing improves usability and can help more people receive the intended message.

Practical practices can include:

- meaningful alternative text for informative images;
- captions or transcripts where appropriate for video/audio;
- descriptive link text;
- clear headings;
- readable font sizes;
- sufficient visual contrast;
- avoiding information that depends only on colour;
- accessible forms and error messages;
- keyboard-friendly interfaces where applicable;
- plain language and scannable structure.

Accessibility obligations vary by jurisdiction and context. A coordinator can support the process but should not claim legal conformance or accessibility certification unless independently established.

## Responsible AI in marketing

AI may be useful for low-risk assistance when the employer permits it.

Appropriate uses can include:

- brainstorming campaign concepts;
- drafting first-pass headlines or captions;
- proofreading non-sensitive copy;
- summarizing public information;
- organizing keyword themes;
- creating fictional practice data;
- generating first-pass checklists;
- suggesting alternative phrasing.

Human review remains necessary.

Do not rely on AI alone for:

- final factual claims;
- prices or current availability;
- legal or compliance requirements;
- regulated health or financial claims;
- statistics without source verification;
- translations for consequential public information;
- targeting decisions involving sensitive data;
- accessibility certification;
- customer-data decisions;
- final brand approvals.

Do not put private customer data, passwords, payment information, API keys, unreleased campaigns, confidential contracts, or protected personal information into an unapproved AI tool.

## Education and entry pathways — United States

BLS reports that the professional occupation benchmark generally requires a **bachelor’s degree**. However, junior marketing assistant and coordinator roles vary considerably. Some employers may accept:

- certificates;
- associate-level study;
- internships;
- customer-service or sales experience;
- office-administration experience;
- ecommerce experience;
- a relevant portfolio;
- demonstrated writing, spreadsheet, social, content, or analytics skills.

A low-cost entry sequence can be:

1. build strong spreadsheet, writing, presentation, and calendar skills;
2. learn marketing fundamentals and the customer journey;
3. create fictional campaign samples with no real customer data;
4. learn basic analytics and reporting;
5. add email, social, web, or ecommerce practice;
6. build a small portfolio showing your process and results from safe practice projects;
7. seek internships, campus work, nonprofit projects, paid assistant roles, or employer training;
8. apply for assistant/coordinator roles while continuing to build specialist skills.

## Income research — keep official statistics separate from market estimates

### United States — official benchmark

O*NET reports **2025 median wages of $37.87/hour and $78,760/year** for Market Research Analysts and Marketing Specialists. The current national distribution shown through O*NET’s BLS wage data is approximately **$43,390 at the 10th percentile, $78,760 median, and $155,480 at the 90th percentile annually**.

BLS reports a **May 2024 median annual wage of $76,950** for the same broad occupation family and projects **7% growth from 2024 to 2034**, with about **87,200 openings per year**.

These are professional occupation-level statistics, not guaranteed pay for a junior coordinator or assistant.

### United States — current non-government estimate

Indeed reported approximately **$57,136/year** average base salary for U.S. Marketing Coordinators, based on about **4.3k salaries from job postings over the prior 36 months**, updated **August 2, 2026**. The displayed range was approximately **$40,235 to $81,136/year**.

This market-title estimate is narrower and generally more junior than the BLS/O*NET professional benchmark. Do not average the two datasets together.

### Canada — official benchmark

Job Bank reports Canada-wide wages for marketing coordinator / NOC 11202, updated **November 19, 2025**, of approximately **C$20.50 low, C$35.58 median, and C$57.44 high per hour**. The reference period is 2023–2024 and the source is the Statistics Canada Labour Force Survey.

### Colombia — current non-government estimate

Indeed reported approximately **COP 3,042,755/month** for Coordinador/a de mercadotecnia in Colombia, updated **May 25, 2026**. The estimate was based on only **2 reported salaries**, so it should be treated as a very small-sample market signal, not a representative national wage.

## Portfolio ideas

Create only fictional or sanitized work unless you have permission to use real material.

A useful portfolio might include:

- a fictional one-month campaign calendar;
- a sample email campaign with subject line, body, CTA, and QA checklist;
- a social-media content plan;
- a landing-page wireframe or content brief;
- a campaign performance dashboard built from fictional data;
- an accessibility review checklist;
- an example UTM naming scheme;
- a campaign approval workflow;
- a short lessons-learned report.

For each artifact, explain the objective, your process, the checks you performed, and what you would measure.

## Interview preparation

Be ready to describe:

- a project you organized from start to finish;
- how you check copy, links, dates, prices, and assets before launch;
- how you would handle an unsubscribe or privacy-related request;
- how you use spreadsheets or dashboards;
- how you respond to an unexpected vendor-payment request;
- how you manage competing deadlines;
- how you protect customer information;
- how you use AI responsibly;
- how you would measure a simple campaign.

If you lack paid marketing experience, use coursework, volunteer work, customer-service examples, ecommerce experience, or fictional portfolio projects and clearly state what was practice rather than employment.

## 30-day starter plan

### Week 1 — fundamentals

- learn the marketing funnel/customer journey;
- practice spreadsheets;
- review email, social, web, and campaign vocabulary;
- study privacy and cybersecurity basics.

### Week 2 — build

- create a fictional campaign brief;
- build a campaign calendar;
- draft sample content;
- create a basic tracking plan.

### Week 3 — measure

- build a small fictional dataset;
- calculate simple rates and trends;
- prepare a one-page dashboard;
- write three lessons learned.

### Week 4 — job search

- revise résumé bullets around coordination, writing, customer service, sales, digital tools, and data;
- organize your portfolio;
- search several job-title variations;
- compare actual requirements before paying for more training;
- verify employer location, schedule, and work authorization requirements before applying.
