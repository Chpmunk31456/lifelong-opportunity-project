# Guide 67 — Current-Source Evidence 02

**Occupation:** Cook and Culinary Specialist  
**Review date:** 2026-08-20  
**Gate:** Current-source Research — PASS

## Research scope and occupation boundary

Guide 67 uses **O*NET-SOC 35-2014.00 — Cooks, Restaurant** as the primary U.S. occupational benchmark because the legacy title “Cook and Culinary Specialist” is broad and non-standardized. O*NET describes restaurant cooks as workers who prepare, season, and cook dishes in restaurants and lists titles such as Cook, Line Cook, Grill Cook, Fry Cook, Prep Cook, Banquet Cook, Breakfast Cook, and Saucier.

The guide must not imply that “culinary specialist” automatically means chef, head cook, kitchen manager, food-service manager, dietitian, nutritionist, or another higher-authority or regulated role. Duties and authority depend on the employer, establishment, jurisdiction, training, and actual job description.

## United States — official occupation, wages, outlook, and work-based learning

### O*NET/BLS 2025 restaurant-cook benchmark

O*NET currently reports BLS 2025 national wage data for `35-2014.00`:

- median hourly wage: **USD $17.98**;
- median annual wage: **USD $37,390**;
- 25th percentile annual wage: **USD $34,010**;
- 75th percentile annual wage: **USD $44,620**;
- 10th percentile annual wage: **USD $28,700**;
- 90th percentile annual wage: **USD $47,900**.

O*NET reports approximately **1,460,200** restaurant cooks employed in 2024 and approximately **250,700 projected job openings** over the 2024–2034 projection period. It classifies the occupation as Bright Outlook and reports projected growth as **7% or higher**.

O*NET task evidence supports core coverage of:

- storing and cooking food at correct temperatures;
- cleaning food-preparation areas and equipment;
- portioning, arranging, and garnishing food;
- checking freshness and rotating stock;
- cooking to recipes or professional judgment;
- weighing and mixing ingredients;
- using ovens, grills, broilers, steam equipment, utensils, and other kitchen tools;
- coordinating kitchen work where delegated.

O*NET lists four example Registered Apprenticeship titles for this occupation: **Chef de Partie (Cook Hotel & Restaurant)**, **Cook**, **Cook (Hotel & Restaurant)**, and **Production Cook**. This supports describing apprenticeship as a possible work-based pathway, not as a universal requirement or guaranteed opening.

### BLS broader Cooks comparison

The BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook currently reports a broader **Cooks** benchmark with median pay of **USD $35,760/year ($17.19/hour)**, about **2,805,100** jobs, projected growth of **5%**, and approximately **130,600 openings per year**. Because BLS groups several cook specialties together, the guide should keep this broader benchmark separate from the more specific O*NET Restaurant Cooks data.

## Current non-government U.S. pay estimate

A current non-government estimate may be included only as a separately labeled market signal:

- **ZipRecruiter, August 4, 2026:** average Cook pay **USD $33,340/year**, approximately **$16.03/hour**, with a reported 25th–75th percentile range of **$28,000–$37,500**.

ZipRecruiter states that its estimates use employer job postings and third-party data. This figure must not be represented as a government statistic and should not be averaged with official O*NET/BLS data.

## United States — food-safety boundary

The FDA states that the **2022 Food Code** is the most recent full Food Code and that it is a **model** for retail and food-service safety, not one nationwide restaurant law. State, local, tribal, territorial, and other authorities decide how provisions are adopted and enforced. FDA published a Supplement to the 2022 Food Code on **November 4, 2024** and subsequently made editorial corrections.

Guide 67 therefore must direct readers to verify the actual controlling health-department and employer requirements before paying for food-handler, food-protection-manager, allergen, or other food-safety training. A cook must never be told that one national credential is automatically required or sufficient everywhere.

Food-safety coverage should include handwashing and hygiene, employee illness reporting, cross-contamination prevention, time/temperature controls, receiving and storage, cleaning and sanitizing, stock rotation, allergen communication, pest control, waste handling, and incident escalation according to applicable rules and employer procedures.

## Worker-safety boundary

Cooking work can involve heat, steam, hot oil, knives, slicers, mixers, ovens, heavy or awkward loads, slippery floors, repetitive movement, rushed work, and crowded kitchens. The Version 2 guide should emphasize training and employer procedures for burns, cuts, lifting, spill response, machine guarding, fire response, protective equipment, and emergency escalation. It must not encourage bypassing food-safety or worker-safety controls to improve service speed.

## Canada — official comparison

The closest national Canadian benchmark is **NOC 63200 — Cooks**. Job Bank describes cooks as preparing and cooking a wide variety of foods in restaurants, hotels, hospitals, health-care institutions, central commissaries, educational institutions, ships, and camps; apprentices are included in the unit group.

Current national Job Bank wage data, updated **November 19, 2025** using the **2023–2024 Labour Force Survey** reference period, reports:

- low: **CAD $15.00/hour**;
- median: **CAD $18.00/hour**;
- high: **CAD $25.00/hour**.

Job Bank states that secondary school is usually required and that a three-year cook apprenticeship, a college/other program in cooking or food safety, or several years of commercial cooking experience may be required. Trade certification is available but voluntary across provinces and territories, and qualified cooks may pursue the Red Seal endorsement after the interprovincial examination.

Canadian requirements must remain province-, territory-, employer-, and establishment-specific.

## Colombia — current public training pathways

SENA Betowa provides current public examples relevant to entry and progression.

### Cocina — Técnico

Current SENA Betowa information lists **COCINA** as a titled (`Titulada`) **Técnico** program, in person, with a duration of **2,200 hours**. The program description states that learners receive and handle raw materials for food preparation to quality standards and support food-service operations under food-safety parameters.

Availability, intake, location, prerequisites, and selection requirements must be checked directly in Betowa at the time of application.

### Cocina básica nivel 1

SENA Betowa lists **COCINA BÁSICA NIVEL 1** as a complementary special course, in person, with a duration of **40 hours**. Listed competencies include controlling raw-material handling according to quality and profitability parameters and preparing food according to customer requests.

This can be presented as a lower-cost or free-first public training example where available, but completion does not guarantee employment, promotion, food-safety authorization, or chef status.

## Latin America pathway

OIT/Cinterfor provides a regional vocational-training knowledge network and publishes materials on occupational safety and health in professional training, including gastronomy. The guide should direct readers toward national public vocational institutions, labor/education ministries, tourism/hospitality institutes, and OIT/Cinterfor-linked institutions rather than implying one regional credential applies across Latin America.

Readers should verify program recognition, total costs, materials/uniforms, transportation, practical-stage requirements, food-handler or health-document requirements, schedule, and credential portability before enrolling.

## Funding, free-first, and work-based learning locators

Guide 67 should preserve a free-first sequence before recommending expensive private culinary schools:

- **CareerOneStop WIOA-Eligible Training Program Finder** for state-approved programs that may qualify for WIOA support for eligible participants;
- local **American Job Centers** for eligibility and workforce-service guidance;
- **Apprenticeship.gov** for actual Registered Apprenticeship openings, noting O*NET’s approved cook apprenticeship titles;
- employer-paid onboarding, food-safety training, cross-training, tuition reimbursement, mentoring, or promotion pathways where available;
- **SENA Betowa** for Colombia public programs;
- OIT/Cinterfor and national vocational institutions for broader Latin American discovery.

WIOA funding, admission, apprenticeship placement, employer reimbursement, certification, and employment are never guaranteed.

## Authority and role boundaries to preserve downstream

A cook or culinary specialist may prepare food, perform mise en place, use equipment, monitor temperatures, rotate stock, receive supplies within delegated authority, clean/sanitize work areas, follow recipes, portion food, assist with inventory, and coordinate routine kitchen tasks.

The guide must not imply that every cook may independently:

- diagnose allergies or medical conditions;
- declare food safe when required controls have not been met;
- override health-department or employer food-safety requirements;
- design therapeutic diets without qualified clinical authority;
- hire/fire or formally discipline employees;
- sign contracts or approve major purchases;
- alter payroll or time records outside authorized procedures;
- access unrestricted employee, customer, payment, or business data;
- represent the employer to regulators unless formally designated;
- conceal food-safety incidents, injuries, harassment, theft, or other reportable events.

## Allergen, privacy, cybersecurity, accessibility, and AI boundaries

The Version 2 guide should explicitly retain these controls:

- use approved ingredient, recipe, and preparation information for allergen questions and escalate uncertainty rather than guessing;
- do not diagnose allergies or make medical claims;
- protect customer, employee, payment, scheduling, inventory, delivery-platform, and business information according to role-based access and employer policy;
- never share POS, scheduling, ordering, or other system credentials;
- verify suspicious vendor, payment, password-reset, QR-code, payroll, or bank-change requests through approved channels;
- support accessible service and workplace practices without making legal or accommodation guarantees;
- do not enter payment-card data, employee records, customer personal information, unpublished recipes or business data, incident reports, credentials, or internal investigations into public AI systems without organizational approval;
- AI must not independently decide whether food is safe, whether an allergen is present, whether a legal requirement applies, or whether a worker/customer has a medical condition;
- human review is required for AI-assisted recipes, menus, training materials, schedules, labels, customer communications, and operational decisions.
