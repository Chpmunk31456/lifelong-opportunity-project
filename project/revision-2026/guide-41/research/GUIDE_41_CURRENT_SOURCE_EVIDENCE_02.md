# Guide 41 — Carpenter and Cabinetmaking Technician

**Current-source evidence intake — August 14, 2026**

## Scope decision

Guide 41 is being reconstructed around two closely related but distinct pathways: (1) carpenter / carpenter-joiner work in construction, renovation, framing, finishing, and installation; and (2) cabinetmaking / bench-carpentry work focused on shop fabrication, cabinetry, millwork, finishing, assembly, and installation. Requirements, certification rules, wages, work settings, and training pathways vary by country and employer, so Version 2 must label these distinctions clearly rather than treating all woodworking roles as interchangeable.

## United States — occupation, wages, outlook, and training

O*NET OnLine classifies Carpenters as occupation 47-2031.00. Current O*NET/BLS data report a 2025 U.S. median wage of **$29.12 per hour / $60,580 per year**, approximately **959,000 workers in 2024**, projected growth of **5% to 6% from 2024 to 2034**, and about **74,100 annual openings** from growth and replacement needs. These are national occupational statistics, not promised starting wages.

O*NET also lists Cabinetmakers and Bench Carpenters as a related occupation. Version 2 should explain that cabinetmaking roles may be shop-based and can have different pay and training patterns from field carpentry.

Registered Apprenticeship is a major U.S. work-based-learning pathway for carpentry. Apprenticeship.gov should be used as the official locator for current paid apprenticeship opportunities. Availability varies by employer and geography; the presence of an apprenticeable occupation does not guarantee a local opening.

For public workforce funding, American Job Centers and CareerOneStop/WIOA Eligible Training Provider resources should be presented as locators rather than as guaranteed funding. WIOA eligibility depends on individual circumstances, local workforce-board rules, provider eligibility, and program approval.

## United States — current non-government market estimates

Private compensation estimates must be kept separate from official occupational medians because adjacent titles cover different work scopes.

Salary.com reported, as of **July 1, 2026**, an average U.S. salary of approximately **$41,709 per year / $20 per hour** for *Cabinetmaker and Bench Carpenter*, with a stated 25th–75th percentile range of approximately **$34,226–$51,809**.

Salary.com separately reported, as of **July 1, 2026**, an average of approximately **$60,290 per year / $29 per hour** for *Cabinet Maker*, with a stated 25th–75th percentile range of approximately **$54,219–$66,817**. Because title definitions and data methodologies differ, these figures should be labeled as non-government market estimates and not blended into the BLS/O*NET carpenter median.

## Canada — requirements, certification, wages, and apprenticeship support

Government of Canada Job Bank classifies carpenters under **NOC 72310**. Current national requirements state that completion of secondary school is usually required and that eligibility for trade certification usually requires either a **three- to four-year apprenticeship** or a combination of **more than four years of work experience** plus relevant high-school, college, or industry courses. Carpenter trade certification is compulsory in Quebec and available but voluntary in other provinces and territories. Qualified carpenters may pursue the Red Seal endorsement through the interprovincial examination process.

Job Bank reports national carpenter wages, updated November 19, 2025, of approximately **CAD $22.00 low / $32.12 median / $44.23 high per hour**. Local rates vary materially, so Version 2 should direct readers to Job Bank location filters before making relocation or training decisions.

Canada's skilled-trades support system currently includes provincial/territorial apprenticeship programs, Employment Insurance support during eligible technical training, tax measures, and the **Canada Apprentice Loan**, which can provide up to **CAD $4,000 per period of technical training**, subject to eligibility. The federal Apprenticeship Incentive Grant and Apprenticeship Completion Grant programs are now closed to new applications; Version 2 must not present those legacy grants as currently open.

The federal Apprenticeship Service historically supported employers hiring first-year apprentices, including Carpenter and Cabinetmaker, but its public page currently states that it is **not accepting applications while renewal work proceeds**. This must be presented as a status-dependent employer-support program rather than an active guaranteed subsidy.

## Colombia — public training and qualification pathways

SENA's current Betowa catalog includes **Básico de carpintería y ebanistería**, a **60-hour in-person complementary program** covering skills such as assembling and installing carpentry work. Enrollment depends on current availability, admissions procedures, and local offerings.

Version 2 should direct Colombian readers first to SENA/Betowa for current carpentry, woodworking, furniture, cabinetmaking, finishing, and occupational-safety offerings before paying private providers. The Ministry of Labour's Sistema Nacional de Cualificaciones and OCUPACOL can be used to explore occupational profiles, qualification pathways, and related job-title terminology where relevant.

## Latin America and the Caribbean

Training and licensing systems vary by country. Version 2 should use OIT/Cinterfor as a regional locator for national vocational-training institutions and competency systems, while directing readers to verify the current national provider, eligibility, costs, and recognition rules in their own country.

Useful public-system examples can include SENA (Colombia), SENCE (Chile), INTECAP (Guatemala), INFOTEP (Dominican Republic), and INEFOP (Uruguay), while avoiding any implication that one regional credential is portable everywhere.

## Safety, accessibility, and responsible technology use

Carpentry and cabinetmaking involve significant safety hazards, including power tools, blades, dust, noise, lifting, working at height, chemicals/finishes, and jobsite traffic. Version 2 should explicitly instruct readers to follow employer and jurisdiction-specific machine guarding, PPE, respiratory protection, fall protection, lockout/tagout, chemical-handling, and training requirements. No guide text should encourage bypassing safety controls to improve speed or productivity.

AI tools may be useful for general study, terminology explanations, practice interview questions, or drafting non-sensitive planning notes, but they must not replace approved measurements, structural calculations, shop drawings, safety procedures, engineering specifications, machine instructions, or employer quality controls. Readers should not paste proprietary drawings, customer information, pricing, credentials, or protected project data into public AI services without authorization.

## Free-first / low-cost learning and employer support

The controlled guide should continue the project's free-first rule: review real local job postings, use public training and employer-provided instruction first, learn measurement, blueprint reading, material properties, tool safety, and basic estimating concepts, and only then evaluate private certificates or schools. Readers should ask employers about paid onboarding, apprenticeship, trainee roles, tool allowances, cross-training, tuition assistance, certification reimbursement, and internal advancement pathways.

Any reimbursement condition should be obtained in writing before spending money because repayment or retention terms may apply.

## Required Version 2 distinctions

Version 2 must:

1. Distinguish construction carpentry from cabinetmaking / bench-carpentry scope.
2. Separate official government wage data from private market estimates.
3. Preserve Canada certification and Red Seal nuances, including Quebec compulsory certification.
4. State that legacy Canadian apprenticeship cash grants are closed, while current loan/EI and other skilled-trades supports may still be available.
5. Include Colombia SENA pathways and Latin America public-system locators.
6. Use apprenticeship and workforce-funding sources as locators, not promises.
7. Include strong machine/tool/dust/fall/chemical safety boundaries.
8. Avoid claims of independent human certification, professional accreditation, legal review, or guaranteed employment outcomes.
