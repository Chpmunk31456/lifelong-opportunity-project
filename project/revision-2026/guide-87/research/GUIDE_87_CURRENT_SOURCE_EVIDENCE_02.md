## Research conclusion

Guide 87 has strong current-source support, but jurisdiction mapping must preserve scope differences rather than force false equivalence.

- **United States:** O*NET-SOC **15-1253.00 — Software Quality Assurance Analysts and Testers** is a direct Bright Outlook match.
- **Canada:** use a **two-anchor design**:
  - analyst-level software QA work → **NOC 21222 — Information systems specialists**;
  - testing-technician execution work → **NOC 22222 — Information systems testing technicians**.

No blocker prevents controlled reconstruction.

## United States — O*NET 15-1253.00

O*NET 15-1253.00 is titled **Software Quality Assurance Analysts and Testers**, is marked **Bright Outlook**, and is updated for 2026.

Its official description centers on developing and executing software tests to identify software problems and causes; testing system modifications before implementation; documenting defects in a bug-tracking system; reporting defects to developers; maintaining known-defect information; and participating in design reviews to provide input on requirements, operational characteristics, product design and schedules.

### Current official tasks

Core O*NET tasks include:

- identify, analyze and document problems with program function, output, screen or content;
- document defects in a bug-tracking system and report them to software developers;
- develop test programs covering areas such as database impacts, software scenarios, regression testing, negative testing, bug retests and usability;
- design test plans, scenarios, scripts or procedures;
- document test procedures for replicability and standards compliance;
- provide usability/functionality recommendations;
- install, maintain or use software-testing programs;
- test system modifications before implementation;
- create or maintain known-defect databases.

These tasks support a revised guide built around evidence, repeatability and release risk rather than the simplistic idea that QA is only “finding bugs.”

## Canada — two-anchor design

The guide title combines **analyst** and **tester** work. Canada Job Bank separates those scopes enough that one NOC should not be forced onto both.

### Analyst-level QA anchor — NOC 21222

Canada Job Bank maps **Software QA (Quality Assurance) Analyst** to **NOC 21222 — Information systems specialists**.

Job Bank describes this group as analyzing, testing and assessing systems requirements; developing and implementing information-systems plans, policies and procedures; and advising on information-systems issues.

Current requirements say that a bachelor's degree in computer science, computer systems engineering, software engineering, business administration or a related discipline, **or** completion of a college program in computer science, is usually required.

National wage values updated November 19, 2025 are:

- low: **C$28.85/hour**;
- median: **C$46.15/hour**;
- high: **C$68.68/hour**.

Job Bank's national summary was modified August 7, 2026. Its current national 2024–2033 demand/supply summary says labour demand and supply are expected to be broadly in line for NOC 21222.

### Tester-technician anchor — NOC 22222

Canada Job Bank maps **Software Tester** to **NOC 22222 — Information systems testing technicians**.

Job Bank describes these workers as executing test plans to evaluate the performance of software applications and information/telecommunications systems.

Current requirements include:

- college program in computer science, computer programming or network administration usually required;
- college or other courses in programming or network administration usually required;
- software-vendor certification or training may be required by some employers.

Current national wages are approximately:

- low: **C$17.50/hour**;
- median: **C$35.00/hour**;
- high: **C$51.28/hour**.

Regulation is not uniform across Canada. Job Bank currently identifies **Information Systems Testing Technicians in Manitoba** as regulated through the Certified Technicians and Technologists Association of Manitoba. The revised guide must tell readers to check the province/territory rather than making a blanket national regulation claim.

## Required professional-content boundaries for the revised guide

### Test basis, requirements and traceability

Testing should start from a defined basis, such as:

- approved requirement;
- acceptance criterion;
- user story;
- interface/API contract;
- design/specification;
- defect fix;
- risk;
- regulatory or policy requirement where assigned;
- documented user workflow.

Tests should be traceable to what they are intended to verify. QA must not silently invent product requirements merely to make a test pass or fail.

### Test cases and expected results

A defensible test can identify:

- objective;
- preconditions;
- environment/build/version;
- test data;
- steps;
- expected result;
- actual result;
- pass/fail/block status;
- evidence;
- cleanup/reset where needed;
- linked requirement/defect.

Expected outcomes should be defined before interpreting the actual outcome when practical.

### Reproducible defect reporting

High-quality defect reports should capture relevant information such as:

- concise title;
- environment/build/version;
- preconditions;
- repeatable steps;
- expected result;
- actual result;
- frequency/reproducibility;
- screenshots/log excerpts or other evidence when safe and useful;
- severity/impact;
- linked requirement/test;
- relevant test data identifier without exposing protected data.

A defect report should describe observed behavior rather than accuse a developer or speculate about root cause without evidence.

### Severity versus priority

The guide must distinguish:

- **severity** — impact of the defect on system/user/business behavior;
- **priority** — urgency/order in which the organization chooses to address it.

Teams define scales differently. QA should follow the organization's definitions and escalation process.

### Test levels and types

Relevant categories can include:

- unit testing (often developer-owned but QA may understand/support it);
- integration testing;
- system testing;
- end-to-end testing;
- acceptance testing;
- functional testing;
- regression testing;
- negative/error testing;
- boundary-value testing;
- compatibility testing;
- API testing;
- usability testing;
- accessibility testing;
- performance/load/stress testing;
- recovery/resilience testing where assigned;
- security testing only within explicit authorization.

The guide must avoid implying that one tester performs every test type or that QA owns every release decision.

### Exploratory versus scripted testing

Scripted testing supports repeatability and traceability. Exploratory testing can discover unexpected behavior through skilled investigation. They are complementary, not opposites.

Exploratory work should still respect scope, environment, data and authorization boundaries.

### Test automation

Automation can improve repeatability and coverage for suitable checks, but “automate everything” is not a defensible goal.

Automation requires:

- reliable selectors/interfaces;
- maintainable code;
- version control;
- review;
- stable test data/environment assumptions;
- meaningful assertions;
- failure diagnostics;
- handling of flaky tests;
- maintenance when the product changes.

Automated test success does not prove the product is defect-free.

### CI/CD and release gates

QA may work with continuous integration/delivery pipelines. Controls should include:

- clear build/version identity;
- controlled automated test suites;
- trustworthy results;
- failure handling;
- approved quality/release criteria;
- artifacts/logs retained as needed;
- explicit authority for override/waiver decisions.

Passing automated tests is evidence, not by itself an authorization to release unless the organization's process says so.

## Security-testing boundary

Functional QA is not automatically penetration testing.

Security testing may include safe checks for authorization, input validation, session behavior or secure defaults when those checks are approved. Intrusive scanning, exploitation, credential attacks, destructive tests or penetration testing require explicit authorization and scope.

NIST SSDF is relevant to integrating security practices into the software development life cycle. OWASP WSTG is a useful security-testing reference for authorized web application/service testing, but QA workers must follow employer scope, rules of engagement and legal/security ownership.

Never use production customer data, credentials or systems outside authorization to demonstrate a security finding.

## Test-data privacy and environment controls

Test environments can contain sensitive information.

Required guidance:

- prefer synthetic or approved masked data where possible;
- do not copy production datasets casually into test environments;
- preserve access controls;
- protect credentials/tokens/keys;
- do not attach protected data unnecessarily to bug trackers;
- sanitize screenshots/logs when required;
- follow retention/deletion rules;
- confirm recipients before sharing evidence;
- report suspected exposure through the incident process.

## Accessibility testing boundary

QA can help verify keyboard use, focus behavior, labels, contrast, forms, error messages, screen-reader compatibility and other accessibility characteristics.

Automated accessibility scanners catch only part of the problem space. Passing an automated scan does **not** establish legal accessibility compliance. Manual/assistive-technology testing and organizational accessibility expertise may be required.

## Responsible AI for QA/testing

Potential policy-approved uses:

- draft test ideas or cases;
- draft automation code;
- explain a stack trace/error;
- create synthetic test data;
- summarize non-sensitive defect history;
- suggest edge cases;
- draft test documentation.

Required controls:

- no protected source code, customer data, credentials, private logs or unreleased product information in unapproved AI tools;
- verify generated test logic, assertions and code;
- do not accept invented requirements or expected behavior;
- do not let AI autonomously close defects or approve releases outside governance;
- inspect generated data for privacy, bias and unrealistic assumptions;
- human accountability remains with the assigned team/member;
- use authoritative product requirements and source documentation as evidence, not AI output.

NIST AI RMF and Generative AI Profile are voluntary risk-management guidance, not release-approval authority.

## Safe portfolio guidance

Portfolio work should use public, licensed, open-source or self-built software and synthetic/non-sensitive data.

Useful portfolio examples:

- test plan for a small public demo application;
- requirement-to-test traceability matrix;
- well-written defect reports;
- API test collection against a public/local demo API;
- automation suite for a self-built/open-source application;
- accessibility review with both automated and manual checks;
- performance-test lab using a local/demo system;
- CI workflow that runs tests and preserves results;
- test-data design using synthetic records.

Do not publish employer source code, proprietary requirements, real customer data, credentials, vulnerability details for systems without authorization or screenshots/logs containing protected information.
