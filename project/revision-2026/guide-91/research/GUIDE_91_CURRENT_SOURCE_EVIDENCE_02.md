# Guide 91 — Current-source Evidence 02

**Guide:** 91 — Cloud Support Specialist  
**Controlled branch:** `revision/guide-00-100-2026`  
**Research review date:** 2026-08-22

## Research conclusion
Cloud Support Specialist is a real market title, but major occupational systems do not provide one perfect universal classification. Guide 91 must use a transparent function-based model.

- **United States primary benchmark:** O*NET-SOC **15-1231.00 — Computer Network Support Specialists**. O*NET explicitly includes **cloud networks** in the occupation description and lists **Cloud Support Specialist** as an approved Registered Apprenticeship title.
- **Canada primary infrastructure/support comparison:** **NOC 22220 — Computer network and web technicians**, appropriate when the job supports cloud/network infrastructure, connectivity, systems and related services.
- **Canada adjacent user-facing comparison:** **NOC 22221 — User support technicians**, appropriate when the job mainly supports users, cloud applications and communications software.
- **Colombia primary infrastructure comparison:** **CUOC 35130 — Técnicos en redes y tecnologías de la información**.
- **Colombia adjacent user/application comparison:** **CUOC 35121 — Técnicos en asistencia y soporte de tecnologías de la información**.

No blocker prevents controlled reconstruction. The guide must tell readers to map the actual duties rather than trust the title alone.

## United States — O*NET 15-1231.00
O*NET 15-1231.00 is updated for 2026 and describes workers who analyze, test, troubleshoot and evaluate LANs, WANs, **cloud networks**, servers and other data communications networks, and perform maintenance to keep networks operating with minimal interruption.

### Relevant current duties
O*NET evidence supports a Cloud Support Specialist guide covering:
- backup of network data;
- security settings and access permissions;
- security measures and analysis;
- network/cloud-connectivity troubleshooting;
- network documentation;
- configuration;
- software installation;
- system-operation testing;
- operational-data analysis;
- performance monitoring;
- technical support for network/application/system issues;
- hardware maintenance where relevant;
- specifications for network operation;
- technology learning and user training.

Cloud support roles may also require incident/ticket handling, logs/metrics review, vendor support engagement, identity troubleshooting, service health investigation and controlled remediation depending on employer scope.

## Canada — two-scope design
Canada does not provide one exact Cloud Support Specialist NOC. Current Job Bank sector evidence says data-centre operators and cloud support specialists are increasingly important and identifies related occupations including NOC 21222, 22220 and 22221. For Guide 91, the most practical support-level anchors are 22220 and 22221.

### Primary infrastructure/cloud-network comparison — NOC 22220
Job Bank describes Computer network and web technicians as establishing, operating, maintaining and coordinating networks, hardware, software and related equipment, and monitoring/optimizing connectivity and performance. Cloud-computing trends are explicitly discussed in current outlook material for this occupation.

Current national wages, updated November 19, 2025:
- low **C$21.00/hour**;
- median **C$36.00/hour**;
- high **C$55.00/hour**.

Current typical requirements:
- college or other program in computer science, network administration, Web technology or related field usually required;
- vendor certification/training may be required by some employers;
- Job Bank says registration with a regulatory body is required in **Saskatchewan** for this NOC.

The national summary was modified August 7, 2026. Three-year prospects vary by province/territory.

### Adjacent user/application support comparison — NOC 22221
Job Bank describes User support technicians as first-line technical support for hardware, computer applications and communications software. Current outlook reporting explicitly notes that cloud computing affects demand for support technicians and that cloud-platform skills can improve prospects.

Current national wages, updated November 19, 2025:
- low **C$20.50/hour**;
- median **C$31.47/hour**;
- high **C$49.00/hour**.

Typical requirements include:
- college program in computer science, computer programming or network administration usually required;
- college or other courses in programming/network administration usually required;
- vendor certification or training may be required by some employers.

Guide 91 should tell readers that infrastructure-heavy cloud support aligns more closely with 22220, while SaaS/application/user support can align more closely with 22221.

## Colombia — two-scope design

### Primary infrastructure/cloud-network comparison — CUOC 35130
OCUPACOL **CUOC 35130 — Técnicos en redes y tecnologías de la información** describes workers who implement, operate and maintain technological infrastructure, data networks, IP-based technologies, connectivity-service software and ICT applications and use information-security diagnostic tools.

Relevant functions include:
- implement, operate, administer and troubleshoot data networks;
- operate/maintain data communications systems;
- identify software/equipment upgrade needs and test solutions;
- install/configure computers, network software, operating systems and applications;
- startup/shutdown operations;
- backups and disaster-recovery operations for computer networks;
- user requirements/support;
- documentation.

Official titles include:
- Técnico administrador de centro de datos;
- Técnico de configuraciones de data center;
- Técnico de soporte de red informática;
- Técnico en redes de computadores;
- Técnico especialista en infraestructura tecnológica;
- Tecnólogo en mantenimiento de infraestructura TIC.

This is the strongest Colombian comparison for cloud-infrastructure support.

### Adjacent user/application support comparison — CUOC 35121
OCUPACOL **CUOC 35121 — Técnicos en asistencia y soporte de tecnologías de la información** covers technical assistance for software, hardware, networks, databases, internet, deployment, installation and maintenance.

Titles include:
- Analista de soporte de aplicaciones;
- Consultor soporte tecnología de la información;
- Técnico de soporte en tecnología de la información;
- Técnico de soporte de sistemas e informática;
- Técnico soporte de internet;
- Técnico soporte mesa de ayuda.

This is useful where cloud support is primarily SaaS/application/user incident support rather than infrastructure administration.

## Cloud Support Specialist versus adjacent roles
The revised guide must distinguish:
- **Help Desk/User Support:** first-line endpoint/application/user support, often broad and less infrastructure-focused;
- **Network Support:** connectivity, network devices, routing, DNS and network troubleshooting, including cloud networks;
- **Cloud Support Specialist:** operational support for cloud services/resources, identity, connectivity, compute/storage, monitoring, incidents and controlled remediation;
- **Cloud Administrator:** may have broader platform configuration/governance/administrative authority;
- **Cloud Engineer:** usually has deeper design/build/automation/IaC responsibility;
- **SRE/DevOps:** generally broader engineering/automation/reliability/software-delivery scope.

Titles overlap. Duties and permission boundaries matter more than labels.

## Shared-responsibility boundary
Cloud providers secure and operate portions of the platform, while customers retain responsibilities that vary by service model, configuration, identity, data and workload. AWS, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud each publish shared-responsibility/shared-fate guidance.

Guide 91 must teach readers to check the exact service and provider documentation rather than memorize one generic responsibility chart.

Managed service does **not** mean:
- the provider configures customer IAM correctly;
- customer data classification disappears;
- application vulnerabilities become the provider's responsibility;
- backups/recovery are automatically correct;
- customer network/firewall rules cannot be misconfigured;
- customer logging/monitoring requirements disappear.

## Account, tenant and resource hierarchy
Cloud support staff should understand concepts such as:
- account/tenant/organization;
- subscription/project/account boundaries;
- resource groups/folders/projects;
- regions and availability zones;
- resource tags/labels;
- billing/cost ownership;
- quotas/service limits.

Exact terminology varies by provider.

## IAM, MFA and least privilege
Required guidance:
- use named identities and approved authentication methods;
- use MFA where required;
- apply least privilege;
- avoid shared accounts where policy prohibits them;
- use temporary/elevated access mechanisms where supported;
- protect service accounts/workload identities;
- never share or paste tokens, keys or secrets into tickets/chat/AI tools;
- verify scope before changing roles or policies;
- log/escalate suspicious access.

Support access does not mean unrestricted authority to view customer data or modify every resource.

## Compute, storage and networking foundations
A support specialist should understand practical concepts around:
- virtual machines/instances;
- containers and managed compute at a conceptual level;
- object/block/file storage;
- virtual networks/VPC/VNet;
- subnets;
- routing;
- security groups/firewall rules;
- load balancers;
- DNS;
- public/private IP addressing;
- VPN/private connectivity;
- ports/protocols;
- latency and packet path;
- certificates/TLS at a support level.

## Troubleshooting workflow
A defensible cloud-support sequence:
1. identify user/business impact;
2. verify resource/account/region/environment;
3. confirm timestamps and recent changes;
4. check provider/service health;
5. review relevant logs, metrics and alerts;
6. isolate identity, network, compute, storage, application or dependency layer;
7. test one hypothesis at a time;
8. use only authorized remediation;
9. verify recovery from the user's/service perspective;
10. document evidence, action and escalation.

Do not make broad production changes simply to “see if it works.”

## Logs, metrics and alerts
Cloud support may use:
- platform activity/audit logs;
- application/system logs;
- network-flow logs;
- authentication logs;
- metrics;
- traces where available;
- alerts;
- dashboards;
- service-health notices.

Logs may contain sensitive data. Follow access, retention, export and sharing requirements.

## Incident and escalation boundaries
Classify impact/urgency according to organizational definitions. Escalate when:
- privileged changes exceed authority;
- customer/security/privacy impact is suspected;
- data loss/corruption may have occurred;
- cross-region or provider incident is suspected;
- recovery objectives are at risk;
- contractual/SLA escalation applies;
- root cause is outside support scope.

Do not promise a root cause before evidence supports it.

## Backup, snapshot and recoverability
A snapshot or successful backup job does **not** prove recoverability. Support staff should understand:
- backup scope;
- retention;
- encryption/access;
- region/account separation where required;
- restore testing;
- RPO and RTO concepts;
- application consistency;
- dependency ordering;
- disaster-recovery ownership.

Replication is not automatically a backup because corruption/deletion can replicate.

## Regions, zones and resilience
Regions and availability zones can reduce certain failure risks, but architecture/resilience depends on the service and configuration. Multi-zone or multi-region design can introduce cost, consistency, latency and operational complexity.

A Cloud Support Specialist should not redesign resilience architecture without assigned authority.

## Change management and production authority
Changes may include:
- firewall/security-group rules;
- IAM permissions;
- instance/cluster configuration;
- scaling;
- storage settings;
- DNS;
- routing;
- certificates;
- service parameters;
- logging/monitoring;
- backups;
- deployments.

Before change, confirm authorization, scope, impact, backup/rollback or forward-fix approach, maintenance window and verification plan according to policy.

## CLI, scripts and infrastructure as code
Cloud CLIs, PowerShell/Bash/Python and IaC tools can make powerful changes quickly.

Controls:
- use approved repositories/devices;
- peer review where required;
- avoid hard-coded secrets;
- test in lower-risk environments when feasible;
- verify target account/region before execution;
- understand plan/diff before applying;
- capture change evidence;
- do not run generated commands blindly.

Cloud support may consume IaC outputs without owning architecture or pipeline authoring.

## Cost awareness and FinOps boundary
Support should recognize cost-impacting conditions such as:
- oversized compute;
- orphaned resources;
- excessive logging/egress;
- storage growth;
- idle resources;
- unexpected scaling;
- premium service use.

Do not terminate or resize production resources solely to save money unless authorized. Cost optimization must consider reliability, performance, contracts and business needs.

## Security boundary
Cloud support should recognize and escalate:
- exposed services;
- unusual authentication;
- leaked credentials/secrets;
- overly broad permissions;
- suspicious API activity;
- unexpected configuration changes;
- malware/cryptomining indicators;
- data-exposure signals.

Do not perform penetration testing, credential attacks or destructive security testing without explicit authorization.

## Privacy, data residency and customer data
Cloud does not eliminate privacy obligations. Support may need to know:
- data classification;
- permitted regions;
- customer/tenant segregation;
- minimum necessary access;
- approved diagnostic exports;
- retention/deletion;
- cross-border restrictions;
- ticket/evidence redaction.

Do not copy production data into personal/local systems for troubleshooting.

## Vendor status and support cases
Provider status pages and vendor support cases can be useful evidence. Record:
- incident/service ID;
- affected region/service;
- provider timestamps;
- case number;
- actions requested/taken;
- customer impact;
- internal escalation.

Provider status information may lag or describe only broad service health; continue verifying actual workload behavior.

## Responsible AI
Potential policy-approved uses:
- explain an error message;
- draft a troubleshooting checklist;
- draft a safe command for review;
- summarize non-sensitive logs/tickets;
- create synthetic practice scenarios;
- explain provider terminology;
- draft documentation.

Required controls:
- no credentials, tokens, keys, protected logs, customer data, private architecture or unreleased information in unapproved AI tools;
- verify commands and target account/region before execution;
- verify provider API/CLI syntax against current official documentation;
- treat AI output as a hypothesis, not authoritative root cause;
- do not allow AI to execute privileged production changes or approve incidents/security exceptions outside governance;
- human accountability remains.

## Accessibility and support communication
Support portals, documentation and troubleshooting instructions should be usable by people with disabilities where within the role's scope. Use clear structure, meaningful labels, keyboard-accessible workflows, readable text and non-color-only status indications. Automated checks do not establish full legal accessibility compliance.

## Safe portfolio guidance
Use free-tier/sandbox/lab environments only when terms and cost controls are understood, or use synthetic diagrams/configuration examples without deploying paid resources.

Safe portfolio examples:
- cloud-support runbook;
- synthetic incident timeline;
- IAM least-privilege exercise using dummy identities;
- simple VNet/VPC/subnet diagram;
- log/metric troubleshooting walkthrough using synthetic data;
- backup/restore checklist;
- cost-anomaly investigation using synthetic billing data;
- provider-status correlation exercise;
- ticket/escalation sample with no real customer data.

Never publish employer/customer cloud accounts, architecture, keys, tokens, logs, vulnerability details or proprietary runbooks.
