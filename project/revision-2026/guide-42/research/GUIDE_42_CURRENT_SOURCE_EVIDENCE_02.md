# Guide 42 — Painter and Coating Worker

**Current-source evidence intake — August 15, 2026**

## Scope decision

Guide 42 must distinguish two related but materially different pathways rather than treating all painting and coating work as one occupation:

1. **Construction and maintenance painter** work on buildings, structures, bridges, machinery, and related surfaces, with field/jobsite surface preparation and application work.
2. **Industrial coating / painting production work** involving spraying, rolling, coating, or finishing machines and processes in manufacturing, refinishing, coating, transportation-equipment, and similar settings.

The pathways overlap in surface preparation, coating application, quality control, PPE, and chemical-safety concepts, but duties, machinery, hazards, training, certification, wages, and licensing vary by setting and jurisdiction. Version 2 must label these distinctions clearly.

## United States — construction and maintenance painters

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook identifies **Painters, Construction and Maintenance (SOC 47-2141)**. BLS reports a **May 2024 median wage of $48,660 per year / $23.40 per hour**, approximately **342,200 jobs in 2024**, projected employment growth of **4% from 2024 to 2034**, and about **28,100 openings per year** over the decade from growth and replacement needs.

BLS states that painters typically learn on the job and that no formal educational credential is typically required for entry. Some specialty painters may need certification. Apprenticeship is an available work-based-learning route, and BLS directs readers to the U.S. Department of Labor apprenticeship system and state/local apprenticeship resources for current opportunities.

These are national occupational statistics, not promised starting wages or guaranteed local openings. Version 2 must direct readers to current state/local data and employers before making training or relocation decisions.

## United States — industrial coating and painting pathway

O*NET OnLine classifies **Coating, Painting, and Spraying Machine Setters, Operators, and Tenders (51-9124.00)** as workers who set up, operate, or tend spraying or rolling machines used to coat or paint products. The occupation includes job titles such as industrial painter, paint technician, powder coater, spray painter, automotive painter, and coater operator.

Current O*NET/BLS-linked data report a **2025 median wage of $23.20 per hour / $48,250 per year**, approximately **165,500 workers in 2024**, projected growth of **1% to 2% from 2024 to 2034**, and about **15,800 projected openings** over the projections period as displayed by O*NET. O*NET lists tasks such as spraying prepared surfaces, monitoring coating defects, adjusting spray equipment, checking viscosity and coating quality, cleaning equipment, applying rust-resistant undercoats, and disposing of hazardous waste appropriately.

Version 2 must not blend the construction-painter and industrial-coating statistics into one median or one outlook.

## Canada — painter/decorator trade pathway

Canada's Red Seal Program identifies **Painter and Decorator (NOC 73112)** as a designated Red Seal trade. The Red Seal description covers preparation and application of decorative and protective finishes in residential, commercial, institutional, and industrial settings, including paint, high-performance coatings, waterproofing, fireproofing, clear finishes, wall coverings, and specialty finishes.

Government of Canada Job Bank wage data for painters and decorators, updated November 19, 2025, report national hourly wages of approximately **CAD $20.00 low / $28.00 median / $40.99 high**. Rates vary materially by province, territory, and region, so Version 2 must direct readers to current Job Bank location filters.

The Red Seal designation does not mean one universal licensing rule applies throughout Canada. Apprenticeship, certification, compulsory-trade status, exam requirements, and recognition vary by province or territory and must be verified with the responsible apprenticeship authority before enrollment or relocation.

## Canada — industrial painter/coater pathway

Government of Canada Job Bank classifies industrial painters, coaters and metal finishing process operators under **NOC 94213**. Job Bank describes industrial painters and coaters as workers who operate/tend machines or use brushes and spray equipment to apply paint, enamel, lacquer, or other non-metallic protective and decorative coatings to products, typically in manufacturing companies and custom refinishing, coating, and plating shops.

Current Job Bank national wage data report approximately **CAD $18.00 low / $24.61 median / $36.00 high per hour** for this occupational group. Job Bank states that the occupation usually requires a secondary school diploma or several weeks of on-the-job training. Prospects vary by province and territory.

These figures must remain separate from the Painter and Decorator trade wage data.

## Colombia — occupation and public training

Colombia's Ministry of Labour OCUPACOL occupational catalog includes **Pintores y empapeladores (CIUO 7131)**. Listed functions include selecting, preparing and mixing paints; applying or spraying paint, varnish and related materials to building surfaces; applying primers and protective paints; extending industrial/protective coatings to metal structures, pipes, bridges and similar surfaces; advising on materials; and assembling/using scaffolding for painting work where applicable.

SENA's current training framework includes **Pintura arquitectónica y acabados especiales** as a construction-sector technical-labor program. SENA has also documented current training activity in architectural painting and special finishes, including programs designed to expand participation in construction trades.

SENA's public employment system has recently carried construction-painter vacancies, illustrating real employer demand, but individual vacancy pay and requirements are local snapshots and must not be presented as national wage benchmarks. Version 2 should direct Colombian readers first to SENA, Agencia Pública de Empleo, OCUPACOL, and the Ministry of Labour for current public training, occupational profiles, and employment information before paying private providers.

## Latin America and the Caribbean — public-system locators

Training and credential systems vary across Latin America and the Caribbean. OIT/Cinterfor documents a regional network of public vocational-training institutions and competency systems, including SENA (Colombia), SENCE (Chile), INTECAP (Guatemala), INFOTEP (Dominican Republic), INEFOP (Uruguay), and other national institutions.

Chile's SENCE currently lists construction training that includes **labores de pintado y barnizado en construcción** and has operated no-cost painter/construction training programs. These examples show that public or subsidized training may exist, but availability, eligibility, hours, stipends, and recognition are program- and location-specific.

Version 2 should therefore use OIT/Cinterfor and national vocational-training agencies as **locators**, not imply that one regional credential is portable across countries or that free training is always open.

## Safety — coatings, solvents, spray finishing, and fire/explosion controls

Painting and coating work can involve hazardous vapors, mists, powders, dusts, residues, and flammable/combustible materials. OSHA 29 CFR **1910.107** sets controls for spray finishing using flammable and combustible materials, including requirements related to designated spraying areas, ventilation/exhaust, ignition-source control, grounding/bonding, spray booths, housekeeping, residue disposal, flammable-liquid handling, powder coating, and fire protection.

Version 2 must not teach readers to improvise spray booths, ventilation, electrical classifications, respiratory protection, or flammable-liquid controls. It should direct workers to employer procedures, product labels/SDS, competent supervision, and jurisdiction-specific regulations.

## Safety — lead and hazardous surface disturbance

OSHA 29 CFR **1926.62** explicitly covers construction work involving occupational exposure to lead, including painting and decorating, removal or encapsulation of lead-containing materials, renovation/repair, manual scraping and sanding, spray painting with lead paint, abrasive blasting, and related activities.

Where lead-containing coatings may be present, Version 2 must use a strong stop/escalate boundary: workers should not assume that ordinary sanding, scraping, blasting, heating, or cleanup is safe. Exposure assessment, engineering/work-practice controls, respiratory protection, protective clothing, hygiene, training, and regulated procedures may be required.

The guide must also warn that asbestos, other hazardous coatings, contaminated substrates, and unknown legacy materials require jurisdiction- and employer-specific assessment before disturbance.

## Safety — respiratory protection and work at height

OSHA 29 CFR **1910.134** establishes respiratory-protection program requirements where respirators are required. Selection, medical evaluation, fit testing, training, cartridge/filter selection, maintenance, and program administration must be handled under the applicable employer/regulatory program; a consumer dust mask or self-selected cartridge must not be presented as a universal solution.

BLS identifies falls from ladders and work at height among painter hazards. Version 2 must direct readers to applicable ladder, scaffold, fall-protection, and competent-person requirements rather than providing shortcuts or unsupervised high-risk procedures.

## Free-first / low-cost learning and employer support

Version 2 should preserve the project's free-first principle:

- review real local employer requirements before buying training;
- use public occupational profiles and safety resources;
- check public vocational-training institutions and registered apprenticeship/work-based-learning programs;
- ask employers about paid onboarding, trainee roles, apprenticeship, PPE, tools, certification reimbursement, tuition assistance, and internal advancement;
- verify any repayment, retention, tool-charge, or reimbursement terms in writing before spending money.

Private certificates should be evaluated only after confirming that local employers or regulators actually value or require them.

## Accessibility and responsible technology use

The occupation can involve prolonged standing, bending, kneeling, reaching, climbing, lifting, fine visual inspection, color matching, respirator use, and work in indoor/outdoor or industrial environments. Version 2 should encourage readers to evaluate actual job demands and request lawful workplace accommodations where appropriate without implying that every setting can accommodate every limitation.

AI tools may help with terminology, study planning, interview practice, checklists, or non-sensitive learning notes, but must not replace SDS instructions, manufacturer technical data sheets, exposure assessments, coating specifications, respirator selection, fall-protection plans, confined-space procedures, electrical classifications, environmental controls, or employer-approved work instructions. Proprietary drawings, formulas, customer data, credentials, incident information, or protected project data should not be pasted into public AI systems without authorization.

## Required Version 2 distinctions

Version 2 must:

1. Distinguish construction/maintenance painting from industrial coating/production work.
2. Keep official wage and outlook statistics separate by occupational classification and country.
3. Treat apprenticeship, Red Seal, certification, licensing, and employer requirements as jurisdiction-specific.
4. Use public training and workforce systems as locators, not guarantees of admission, funding, employment, or wage outcomes.
5. Include Colombia SENA/OCUPACOL pathways and broader Latin American public-system locators.
6. Include strong controls for lead/legacy coating disturbance, respiratory hazards, spray-finishing fire/explosion hazards, ladders/scaffolds/fall protection, PPE, SDS/chemical communication, waste handling, and stop/escalate decisions.
7. Avoid unsupervised procedural instruction for hazardous blasting, spray finishing, confined spaces, respiratory selection, or legacy-material removal.
8. Avoid claims of independent human certification, legal review, environmental approval, licensing determination, accessibility certification, guaranteed employment, or guaranteed earnings.
