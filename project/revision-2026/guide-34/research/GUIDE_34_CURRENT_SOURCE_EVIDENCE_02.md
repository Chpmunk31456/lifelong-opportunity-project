## Research purpose and control boundary

This record refreshes the legacy Guide 34 evidence before English Version 2.0 reconstruction. It separates official occupational, safety, workforce, training, and public-institution sources from professional-association credential information and clearly labeled non-government compensation estimates.

The guide must not imply that one title, credential, course, or training route applies to every manufacturing sector. Quality inspection duties, product standards, metrology requirements, regulated-industry requirements, employer qualifications, and legal duties vary by product, process, jurisdiction, and employer. Readers must verify current requirements before paying for training, accepting safety-critical work, relocating, or relying on a credential.

No source in this record constitutes independent human certification of the guide, professional translation certification, accreditation of this publication, legal advice, safety approval, or a guarantee of employment, funding, admission, certification, or earnings.

## 1. United States occupation scope, entry requirements, pay, and outlook

### U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Quality Control Inspectors

Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook, **Quality Control Inspectors**.  
URL: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/production/quality-control-inspectors.htm  
Page last modified: August 28, 2025; verified August 12, 2026.

Controlled facts suitable for Guide 34:

- BLS describes quality control inspectors as workers who examine products and materials for defects or deviations from specifications.
- Typical work can include reading specifications and blueprints, monitoring production operations, inspecting/test-measuring materials and products, accepting or rejecting items, recording inspection data, and discussing results with production workers and supervisors.
- BLS states that quality control inspectors typically need a high school diploma for entry and receive on-the-job training. Exact employer requirements vary with industry, equipment, and product complexity.
- Median annual wage was **$47,460 in May 2024**. The lowest 10 percent earned less than **$34,590**, and the highest 10 percent earned more than **$75,510**.
- The manufacturing-industry median shown by BLS was **$48,170** in May 2024.
- Employment is projected to show **little or no change (0 percent) from 2024 to 2034**.
- BLS projects about **69,900 openings per year**, on average, over the decade, largely from replacement needs.
- BLS notes that automation, including 3D scanning, can increase inspection productivity, while inspections and validation remain necessary for products requiring human judgment or testing.

Controlled editorial implication:

The English master should distinguish a production-floor quality inspector role from broader quality assurance, quality engineering, laboratory, regulatory, or supervisory roles. It should also explain that “manufacturing technician” is an employer-defined umbrella title and can include production, testing, troubleshooting, metrology, process support, maintenance support, documentation, or quality tasks depending on the employer.

## 2. Safety controls for inspection and manufacturing work

### OSHA — Control of Hazardous Energy (Lockout/Tagout), 29 CFR 1910.147

Source: Occupational Safety and Health Administration, **1910.147 — The control of hazardous energy (lockout/tagout)**.  
URL: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147  
Verified August 12, 2026.

Controlled facts:

- The standard addresses servicing and maintenance where unexpected energization, startup, or release of stored energy could cause injury.
- Covered employers must establish an energy-control program with procedures, employee training, and periodic inspections.
- Lockout/tagout is not interchangeable with ordinary production-machine guarding; whether a task is servicing/maintenance or normal production matters to the applicable control.
- Guide 34 must not tell an inspector or technician to bypass guards, interlocks, or employer energy-control procedures to obtain a measurement, inspect a part, clear a jam, reach into a danger zone, clean equipment, or troubleshoot equipment.
- Only workers trained and authorized for the applicable task should perform energy-isolation activities required by the employer and law.

### OSHA — machine guarding relationship

Sources: OSHA machine-guarding and lockout/tagout materials.  
URLs:  
- https://www.osha.gov/machine-guarding  
- https://www.osha.gov/etools/lockout-tagout/hot-topics/relationship-subpart-o/normal-production  
Verified August 12, 2026.

Controlled interpretation:

- OSHA distinguishes safeguards used during normal production from energy-control requirements that can apply during servicing or maintenance.
- The guide should teach hazard recognition and escalation, not machine-specific bypass, repair, maintenance, or energized diagnostic procedures.
- PPE, guarding, chemical controls, ergonomics, electrical safety, respiratory protection, and other requirements depend on the workplace and task. The guide must direct readers to employer procedures, safety data sheets, equipment instructions, and applicable regulations.

### Employer-supported learning

A practical low-cost route can include paid entry-level production or inspection work followed by employer training in measurement tools, drawings/specifications, defect documentation, statistical process control, quality systems, or sector-specific requirements. Readers should ask employers about paid onboarding, tuition reimbursement, credential reimbursement, tool/PPE allowances, cross-training, and progression pathways. These benefits are employer-specific and must be verified in writing.

## 5. Professional credentials — ASQ boundaries

Sources: American Society for Quality (ASQ).  
URLs:  
- https://www.asq.org/cert/quality-inspector  
- https://www.asq.org/cert/quality-technician  
- https://www.asq.org/cert/catalog/inspector-technician  
Verified August 12, 2026.

Controlled facts:

- ASQ offers the **Certified Quality Inspector (CQI)** and **Certified Quality Technician (CQT)** credentials.
- ASQ describes CQI work as supporting quality engineers, supervisors, or technicians through inspection, measurement, documentation, process-performance recording, and reporting.
- As of the verification date, ASQ lists the CQI exam fee as **$460** and requires **three years of full-time paid on-the-job experience** in one or more CQI Body of Knowledge areas, with specified education waivers that can reduce the experience requirement.
- ASQ’s credential pages and exam fees can change. The guide should link to the current ASQ page rather than presenting a credential as mandatory.

Controlled credential boundary:

CQI/CQT are professional certifications, not universal licenses to work as a quality inspector or manufacturing technician. Employers, industries, government regulators, customers, contracts, and standards can require other qualifications. The guide must not imply that ASQ certification substitutes for employer authorization, regulatory qualification, product-specific certification, or safety training.

## 6. Canada: occupation mapping, wages, and training caution

### Government of Canada Job Bank

Quality-control titles in Canada map to different NOC groups depending on the product and manufacturing sector. There is no single national wage figure that safely represents every quality-control-inspector title.

Examples verified August 12, 2026:

- **Quality control inspector — plastic products manufacturing** maps to NOC 94212 in Job Bank. Job Bank’s Canada wage page, updated November 19, 2025, reports national hourly wages of **CAD $17.77 low, $21.91 median, and $30.00 high** for this title/occupation mapping.  
  URL: https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/wages-occupation/12843/ca
- **Inspectors and testers in mineral and metal processing** are a different occupational group; Job Bank’s current summary shows a national median of **CAD $26.24/hour** for the example “mineral sampler — mineral products processing.”  
  URL: https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/summary-occupation/13075/ca

Controlled Canada rule:

The guide must tell readers to identify the correct Job Bank occupation/NOC for the actual product sector before using wage, outlook, or qualification data. It should not merge plastic, food, textile, vehicle, metal/mineral, aerospace, pharmaceutical, or other inspection occupations into one Canadian wage claim.

Training and work-based learning are province/territory and occupation dependent. Apprenticeship should be presented as a possible route for related manufacturing trades, not as a universal requirement or universally available quality-inspector apprenticeship. Readers should verify with the applicable provincial/territorial apprenticeship authority, school, employer, and Job Bank profile.

## 10. Technology, automation, AI, and data controls

BLS specifically notes that automation can reduce time required for some inspections, including through 3D scanners, but cannot replace all inspection and validation tasks. The guide should therefore emphasize transferable skills: measurement and metrology, reading specifications/drawings, documentation, sampling, basic statistics/SPC, root-cause participation, traceability, communication, and safe use of digital inspection systems.

If AI or computer-vision tools are used, the guide must not tell readers to treat model output as final product acceptance when the employer, customer, procedure, or regulation requires qualified human review. Workers should follow approved procedures for calibration, validation, traceability, access control, proprietary data, personally identifiable information, cybersecurity, and escalation of anomalous results. Do not upload confidential drawings, customer data, inspection images, or production records into unapproved public AI services.

## 11. Accessibility and inclusive pathways

Quality/manufacturing work can involve standing, visual inspection, fine measurement, noise, PPE, shift work, or handling parts, but the exact essential functions vary by job. The guide should avoid assuming that one physical profile is required for all quality roles. Readers may explore accommodations, accessible tools, alternate measurement interfaces, ergonomic changes, schedule changes, or adjacent documentation/data/quality-system roles according to employer processes and applicable law.

The guide must not diagnose disability, promise an accommodation, or determine legal eligibility. It should encourage verification through the employer and appropriate public disability-employment or vocational-rehabilitation resources.
