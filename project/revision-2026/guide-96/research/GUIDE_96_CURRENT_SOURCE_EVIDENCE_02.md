## United States — official occupation and wage evidence

### O*NET OnLine — U.S. Department of Labor
Sources:
- https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/17-3023.00
- https://www.onetonline.org/search/task/choose/17-3023.00

Controlled facts:
- Current occupation: **17-3023.00 — Electrical and Electronic Engineering Technologists and Technicians**; O*NET page updated in 2026.
- O*NET defines the occupation as applying electrical/electronic theory and related knowledge, usually under engineering-staff direction, to design, build, repair, adjust, and modify electrical components, circuitry, controls, and machinery for evaluation and engineering use.
- Representative job titles include Electrical Engineering Technician, Electronics Engineering Technician, Electrical Technician, Electronics Technician, Engineering Technologist, Communications Technologist, System Technologist, and Engineering Technician.
- Current tasks include maintaining/repairing electronic systems, replacing defective components, using test equipment to diagnose electronic assemblies/systems, reading blueprints/wiring diagrams/schematics, resolving equipment malfunctions, and assembling electrical systems or prototypes.
- O*NET places the occupation in **Job Zone Three — Medium Preparation Needed**. It notes that vocational-school training, related experience, or an associate degree is common and that one to two years of training/on-the-job experience is typical.
- O*NET lists approved U.S. apprenticeship titles including Electrical Technician, Electronics Technician, Instrumentation Technician, Instrumentation & Controls Technician, Calibration Laboratory Technician, and related titles. Availability is local and must be verified.

### O*NET / BLS 2025 occupation-specific wage control
Source: https://www.onetonline.org/link/localwages/17-3023.00

Controlled facts:

- Hourly distribution at the same percentiles: **$23.80; $29.62; $37.59 median; $46.95; $55.62**.

### U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupational Outlook Handbook
Source: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/electrical-and-electronics-engineering-technicians.htm

Controlled facts:

- Employment is projected to grow **1% from 2024 to 2034**.

- Workers may be employed in offices, laboratories, factories, utilities, manufacturing, engineering services, and other technical environments.
- The revised guide should not blend the May-2024 OOH median with the newer 2025 occupation-specific wage table; dates and source context must remain explicit.

## Canada — official occupation, wages, requirements, and training supports

### Job Bank — NOC 22310
Sources:
- https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/summary-occupation/18017/ca
- https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/occupation/18017/ca
- https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/wages-occupation/18017/ca
- https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/requirements/18017/ca

Controlled facts:
- Canadian classification: **NOC 22310 — Electrical and electronics engineering technologists and technicians**.
- Job Bank describes the work as independent technical work or support/services in design, development, testing, production, and operation of electrical/electronic equipment and systems.
- Typical employers include electrical utilities, communications companies, electrical/electronic equipment manufacturers, consulting firms, governments, and manufacturing/processing/transportation industries.
- National wages updated **November 19, 2025**: **C$24.04/hour low, C$35.58/hour median, C$55.34/hour high**. National reference period is 2023–2024, Statistics Canada Labour Force Survey.
- Technologists usually require a two- or three-year college program in electrical/electronics engineering technology or an equivalent; technicians usually require a one- or two-year college program.
- Certification is available through provincial associations and may be required for some positions; supervised work experience is normally required before certification.
- Title regulation varies by province. Job Bank specifically identifies regulated title/practice considerations in jurisdictions such as Québec and Saskatchewan. The guide must direct readers to verify the exact provincial regulator before relying on a title or credential.

## Colombia — official occupation and training pathway

Source: https://ocupacol.mintrabajo.gov.co/Profile/OccupationalProfile/31141

Controlled facts:
- Colombian classification: **CUOC 31141 — Técnicos en electrónica**, competence level 3.
- OCUPACOL describes technical support in electronic/electrical engineering research, design, development, manufacturing, assembly, construction, integration, programming, operation, testing, inspection, maintenance, and repair of electronic equipment/components.
- Listed roles include Técnico de electrónica, Técnico de electrónica industrial, Técnico de ingeniería electrónica, Técnico de ingeniería de instrumentación, Técnico de mantenimiento electrónico, Tecnólogo en electrónica, and Tecnólogo en mantenimiento electrónico e instrumental industrial.

- OCUPACOL labour indicators are useful contextual signals, but the controlled guide should not present the site’s displayed occupation indicators as a guaranteed or universally representative wage benchmark without methodology/date context.

## Electrical safety and professional-scope controls

### OSHA 29 CFR 1910.333 — selection and use of work practices
Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.333

Controlled safety facts:
- Live parts to which an employee may be exposed generally must be **deenergized before work on or near them**, unless the employer demonstrates an exception based on increased hazard or infeasibility.
- Parts that have been deenergized but not locked/tagged according to the standard must be treated as energized.
- Stored electrical energy that could endanger personnel must be released; OSHA explicitly addresses **capacitors and high-capacitance elements**.
- Before equipment is considered deenergized, a **qualified person** must verify that it cannot be restarted and use test equipment to verify the exposed circuit elements/equipment parts are deenergized, including checking for induced voltage/backfeed.
- Only **qualified persons** may work on exposed energized circuit parts/equipment under the energized-work provisions.

### OSHA 29 CFR 1910.334 — test instruments and equipment
Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.334

Controlled safety facts:
- Only **qualified persons** may perform testing work on electric circuits or equipment.
- Test instruments, leads, probes, cables, connectors, and cords must be visually inspected; damaged items must be removed from service.
- Test equipment/accessories must be rated for the circuits/equipment and designed for the environment in which they are used.

### Editorial safety rule
The guide must not teach a general learner to probe energized circuits, defeat interlocks, discharge capacitors, verify absence of voltage, or perform installation/maintenance work beyond employer authorization, training, applicable law, and qualified-person requirements. Hands-on practice should default to deenergized, low-energy, training-lab, simulation, or instructor-supervised environments specifically designed for learning.

## Cybersecurity, connected equipment, and sensitive information

### CISA Secure Our World
Source: https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world

Controlled use:
- CISA promotes phishing recognition/reporting, strong unique passwords/password managers, multifactor authentication, and prompt software updates.
- Electronic/industrial systems may include networked controllers, firmware, diagnostic laptops, configuration files, credentials, equipment inventories, and operational data. Workers should use employer-approved accounts, repositories, removable-media rules, patch/update procedures, backups, least privilege, and change control.
- Do not connect unauthorized devices/software to production systems, bypass security controls, reuse shared credentials, or upload proprietary schematics/configurations to personal cloud or unapproved AI services.

## Responsible AI

### NIST AI Risk Management Framework and Generative AI Profile
Sources:
- https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework
- https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence

Controlled use:
- NIST’s AI RMF is voluntary guidance for managing AI risk; the Generative AI Profile adapts that framework to generative-AI risks.
- AI may help with low-risk learning tasks such as explaining terminology, generating practice questions, organizing non-sensitive notes, drafting checklists, or proposing test cases for review.
- Consequential technician work requires verification against approved schematics, manufacturer documentation, measurements, calibration records, specifications, employer procedures, and qualified engineering/safety direction.
- AI must not be treated as a measurement instrument, protection relay, calibration standard, engineering sign-off, or authority for energized-work decisions.
- Do not upload confidential schematics, credentials, firmware, proprietary code/configurations, critical-infrastructure details, personal data, or client information into unapproved AI systems.

## Accessibility and learning design controls
The revised guide should:
- use short sections, checklists, consistent terminology, and concrete milestones;
- explain symbols/acronyms before heavy use;
- offer simulation, bench, documentation, QA, and supervised-lab alternatives where field hazards or dexterity/vision/hearing barriers exist;
- distinguish accommodation from removal of essential safety/technical requirements;
- avoid implying that disability accommodation authorizes work outside qualification, licensing, or safety rules.

## Editorial controls for the English revision
The revised master should:

1. anchor U.S. scope to O*NET-SOC 17-3023.00, Canada to NOC 22310, and Colombia to CUOC 31141 without claiming perfect cross-country equivalence;
2. distinguish engineering-technician support from licensed professional-engineering authority, electrician/installation authority, inspection/certification authority, and energized-work qualification;
3. use the 2025 occupation-specific O*NET/BLS wage table as the primary U.S. wage control and label the 2024 OOH and Indeed figures by date/source;
4. include Canadian national wages and province-specific title/certification caution without promising recognition;
5. include Colombia’s CUOC electronics scope and the current SENA technologist pathway, while treating complementary courses as supplemental only;
6. provide U.S. WIOA, Canada labour-market agreements, Colombia SENA, OIT/Cinterfor, SENAI, and SENATI as training/funding locators rather than guaranteed seats/funding;
7. teach schematic reading, measurements, components, test planning, calibration awareness, troubleshooting logic, documentation, QA/QC, prototyping, maintenance, instrumentation, automation, and change control in tool-neutral language;
8. explicitly cover electrical shock, arc/energization, stored-energy/capacitor, battery, thermal, ESD, soldering/fume, rotating-equipment, and industrial-environment hazards at an awareness level without operationalizing unsafe energized work;
9. require qualified-person/employer procedures for energized testing, deenergization verification, lockout/tagout, and any work whose safety depends on electrical authorization;
10. include cybersecurity for connected instruments/controllers, firmware/configurations, credentials, removable media, and industrial systems;
11. permit AI only as low-risk assistance with human verification and confidentiality controls;
12. repair the legacy **`Action Plan 1 To 6: False`** defect with a concrete six-step action plan and checkable milestones;
13. maintain plain-language, dysgraphia-friendly structure and avoid unnecessary jargon density;
14. avoid claims of independent human certification, accreditation, legal review, licensed-professional review, accessibility certification, guaranteed wages, guaranteed employment, or guaranteed admission/funding.
