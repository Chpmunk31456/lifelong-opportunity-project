## Localization controls

1. all 19 numbered sections and their order;
2. the non-legal-advice and non-certification disclaimers;
3. the distinction between administrative and substantive legal-support work;
4. the rule that ordinary paralegal status does not create independent authority to practice law;
5. the distinction among education certificate, individual certification, ABA program approval, institutional accreditation, state regulation/registration, and limited legal-service licensure;
6. California’s state-specific regulatory exception exactly as a U.S.-specific claim, not a Latin American rule;
7. the state-specific nature of Arizona, Minnesota, Oregon, and Utah limited-license examples;
8. Canada NOC 42200 and the province-specific licensing/scope caution;
9. Colombia CUOC 34113 occupational mapping and the warning not to use old OCUPACOL 2020–2021 market figures as current wage evidence;
10. SENA Asistencia Administrativa as a transferable foundation only, never legal authorization;
11. SNIES / Registro Calificado verification language;
12. all controlled numerical values and their currencies, dates, and source distinctions;
13. FAFSA, American Job Center, Section 127, and employer-plan eligibility cautions;
14. confidentiality, privilege, work product, cybersecurity, redaction, credential, and records controls;

## Translation policy

Localization may improve natural phrasing, sentence order, punctuation, and terminology for the target language, but may not broaden legal authority, strengthen uncertain claims, convert examples into universal rules, remove caveats, change controlled quantities, or create new credential claims.

Legal occupational titles do not map perfectly across jurisdictions. Where direct equivalence would imply a legal status not established by the source, preserve the source concept and explain it rather than inventing a local professional title.
