## United States — occupation, pay, outlook, and training

### U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics
Source: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/industrial-machinery-mechanics-and-maintenance-workers-and-millwrights.htm

Current BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook evidence states that industrial machinery mechanics, machinery maintenance workers, and millwrights install, maintain, and repair factory equipment and other industrial machinery. BLS reports:

- 2024 employment: about **538,300** across the combined occupation group;
- May 2024 median pay: **$63,510 per year / $30.53 per hour** for the combined group;
- detailed May 2024 medians: **$63,760** for industrial machinery mechanics, **$60,500** for machinery maintenance workers, and **$65,170** for millwrights;
- combined-group lowest 10 percent: less than **$44,430**; highest 10 percent: more than **$91,620**;
- projected combined employment growth: **13% from 2024 to 2034**, much faster than the all-occupation average shown by BLS;
- about **54,200 openings per year**, on average, over the decade, largely including replacement needs;
- typical entry education: high school diploma or equivalent;
- industrial machinery mechanics and machinery maintenance workers commonly need at least a year of on-the-job training, while most millwrights learn through a 3- or 4-year apprenticeship;
- some mechanics and millwrights complete 2-year industrial-maintenance associate programs covering topics such as welding, mathematics, hydraulics, and pneumatics.

BLS also describes increased use of computerized diagnostic systems, vibration analysis, automated manufacturing machinery, conveyors, controls, and related equipment. These points support v2.0 coverage of predictive/condition-based maintenance, digital diagnostics, CMMS/data literacy, and careful human verification rather than treating AI output as authoritative maintenance instruction.

**Interpretation control:** BLS national medians are not entry-level offers, local wage guarantees, or forecasts for any individual worker.

## United States — worker safety and task boundaries

### OSHA control of hazardous energy (lockout/tagout)
Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147 covers servicing and maintenance where unexpected energization, startup, or release of stored energy could cause injury. The standard requires an employer energy-control program with procedures, training, and periodic inspections and addresses electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, chemical, thermal, and other hazardous energy sources within its scope.

### OSHA lockout/tagout tutorial
Source: https://www.osha.gov/etools/lockout-tagout/tutorial

OSHA emphasizes shutting down equipment, isolating energy sources, and preventing release of hazardous energy during covered service or maintenance. Guide 28 must tell readers to follow employer/site procedures, applicable law, equipment documentation, guarding requirements, and authorized-worker rules. It must not teach readers to bypass interlocks, guards, lockout/tagout, qualified-person requirements, or other safety controls.

**Safety boundary:** General career education cannot substitute for employer training, site-specific energy-control procedures, qualified supervision, manufacturer instructions, or jurisdiction-specific occupational-safety requirements.

## Canada — occupation, wages, apprenticeship, and Red Seal

### Government of Canada Job Bank — NOC 72400
Source: https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/summary-occupation/14719/ca

Job Bank maps **Construction millwrights and industrial mechanics** to **NOC 72400** and describes work installing, maintaining, troubleshooting, overhauling, and repairing stationary industrial machinery and mechanical equipment. The page lists college or apprenticeship as a usual requirement category and reports a national median wage of **C$37.00/hour**. Job Bank wage data should be checked by province/territory and region before making a local decision.

### Red Seal — Industrial Mechanic (Millwright)
Source: https://red-seal.ca/eng/trades/indust-mech-mill.shtml

The Red Seal Program currently maintains **Industrial Mechanic (Millwright)** as a Red Seal trade. Its current trade page links the occupational standard, exam information, Skills for Success material, apprenticeship data, and provincial/territorial pathways. Current exam information includes preventative and predictive maintenance, hydraulics, pneumatics/vacuum, conveying systems, safety functions, commissioning/decommissioning, and CMMS among relevant knowledge/task areas.

**Jurisdiction control:** Apprenticeship registration, compulsory/voluntary trade status, certification, exam eligibility, ratios, schooling, and hours are administered through provinces and territories and must be verified where the reader intends to work. Red Seal information must not be presented as a universal legal license.

### Canada apprenticeship support locator
Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/apprentice-service-program.html

The federal Apprenticeship Service identifies Industrial Mechanic (Millwright) among eligible Red Seal trades within its employer/apprentice-support framework. ## Funding and cost-control principles for v2.0

Guide 28 should use a **free-first** decision sequence:

1. Verify local occupation requirements and safety boundaries before paying for training.
2. Check employer-paid onboarding, OEM/vendor instruction, incumbent-worker training, union/industry programs, Registered Apprenticeship or local apprenticeship, public workforce funding, and public vocational programs first.
3. In the U.S., check American Job Center/WIOA eligibility and any employer Section 127 educational-assistance plan before self-funding.
4. In Canada, check provincial/territorial apprenticeship authorities, Red Seal trade information, employment/training services, and current federal/provincial support.
5. In Colombia, check SENA/Betowa and SENA APE before paying a private provider for equivalent foundational training.
6. Compare total cost, tools/PPE, travel, unpaid time, credential recognition, refund terms, completion outcomes, and employer acceptance in writing before enrollment.
7. Avoid programs that promise guaranteed jobs, guaranteed earnings, guaranteed certification, or legal authorization to perform regulated/safety-critical tasks.
