# Guide 84 — Current-Source Evidence 02

## Research decision

Guide 84 is anchored to current official occupational evidence for the United States, Canada and Colombia, supplemented by clearly labelled current non-government U.S. market context. The controlled revision must disclose important classification differences rather than implying that every country's code or wage series describes an identical BI-only population.

## United States — primary benchmark

### O*NET-SOC 15-2051.01 — Business Intelligence Analysts

O*NET OnLine identifies **15-2051.01 — Business Intelligence Analysts** as a **Bright Outlook** occupation, updated 2026. O*NET describes the work as producing financial and market intelligence by querying data repositories and generating reports, and devising methods for identifying patterns and trends in available information sources.

Representative O*NET tasks include:

- generating standard or custom reports for executives, managers, clients and other stakeholders;
- maintaining BI tools, databases, dashboards, systems or methods;
- managing the timely flow of BI information;
- providing technical support for existing reports and dashboards;
- identifying industry or geographic trends with business-strategy implications;
- documenting report/dashboard specifications;
- creating BI tools or systems, including related databases, spreadsheets and outputs;
- collecting business-intelligence data from available sources;
- testing intelligence for consistency with defined needs;
- synthesizing BI/trend data to support recommendations.

### Preparation

O*NET places the occupation in **Job Zone Four — Considerable Preparation Needed**. It states that most occupations in this zone require a four-year bachelor's degree, but some do not, and that considerable related skill/experience and work-related training may be needed.

Current O*NET education responses for new hires show:

- **68% bachelor's degree**;
- **23% master's degree**;
- **5% associate degree**.

These are respondent-reported preparation patterns, not a universal legal requirement for every BI job.

O*NET lists one approved example Registered Apprenticeship title:

- **Business Intelligence Engineer**.

An approved title does not guarantee a current opening in a reader's location.

## Canada — NOC 21221

### Business Intelligence Analyst - Information Technology (IT)

Canada Job Bank currently maps the IT-oriented title **Business Intelligence Analyst - Information Technology (IT)** to **NOC 21221 — Business systems specialists**.

Job Bank describes Business systems specialists as performing detailed system tests, producing reports and developing system architectures, while identifying company strategic objectives and translating them into achievable tasks for IT departments. This mapping is broader than dashboard/reporting-only BI work, so the controlled guide must describe it as a Canadian comparison rather than exact title equivalence.

Current employment requirements state:

- a bachelor's degree in computer science, business administration, information systems or a related discipline, **or** completion of a college program in computer science, is usually required;
- software-vendor certification or training may be required by some employers;
- according to Job Bank records, this occupation is **not regulated in Canada**.

Current national wages, updated **November 19, 2025**, reference period 2023–2024:

- low: **C$30.67/hour**;
- median: **C$45.13/hour**;
- high: **C$62.50/hour**.

Job prospects vary by province/territory. The guide must direct readers to live regional Job Bank information rather than imply one national outlook applies everywhere.

## BI technical and analytical boundaries

The controlled guide must teach that a BI analyst should not:

- silently alter source records to make a dashboard look correct;
- redefine a KPI without owner/governance approval;
- hide filters, exclusions or time windows that materially change interpretation;
- confuse correlation with causation;
- present a forecast, estimate or AI-generated narrative as observed fact;
- suppress outliers solely because they are inconvenient;
- expose row-level sensitive data merely because the analyst can query it;
- bypass access controls to complete analysis;
- claim accounting, legal, clinical or regulatory conclusions outside assigned expertise;
- publish a dashboard before reconciliation/validation against authoritative sources.

Required good practice includes source lineage, reproducible transformations, defined metrics, validation, documented assumptions, stakeholder review, appropriate access controls and correction/version history.

- privacy/security/access control;

- accessibility/inclusive visualization;
- ethical portfolio using synthetic/public/authorized data.

## Responsible AI and automation

AI may help draft SQL, suggest formulas, summarize non-sensitive results, create test cases or propose narrative wording, but the guide must require:

- approved systems/data only;
- no confidential data, credentials or protected extracts in unapproved public AI services;
- validation of AI-generated SQL, calculations, DAX/formulas and narratives;
- reconciliation back to authoritative data;
- distinction between observed facts and generated interpretation;
- review for systematic error, bias and unsupported causal claims;
- no autonomous publication of decision-critical dashboards/narratives without required human approval.

NIST AI RMF and its Generative AI Profile provide voluntary risk-management guidance; they do not replace organizational data governance.

## Privacy, cybersecurity and access

BI analysts commonly handle sensitive commercial, employee, customer, financial or operational data. The guide must preserve:

- least-privilege access;
- approved data exports/storage;
- protection of credentials/secrets;
- row-level/security-role concepts;
- secure sharing;
- approved retention/deletion;
- incident reporting for unauthorized disclosure;
- no production/security architecture authority implied merely by having BI access.

## Accessibility

Dashboards and reports should consider keyboard access where supported, meaningful labels, readable text/contrast, non-color-only encoding, sensible reading/order structure, table alternatives where appropriate and accessible electronic-document concepts. Automated checking does not constitute complete legal accessibility certification.
