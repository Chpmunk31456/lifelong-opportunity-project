# Guide 81 — Current Source Evidence 02

**Occupation:** Health Information Technologist and Medical Registrar  
**Stage:** Current-source Research  
**Research date:** 2026-08-21  
**Controlled branch:** `revision/guide-00-100-2026`

## Research conclusion

Guide 81 has unusually strong international occupational alignment:

- United States: **O*NET-SOC 29-9021.00 — Health Information Technologists and Medical Registrars**;
- Canada: **NOC 12111 — Health information management occupations**;
- Colombia: **CUOC 32520 — Técnicos en documentación sanitaria**.

These sources support a guide centered on health-record systems, coding/classification, data quality, registry work, confidentiality, reporting and health-information operations. The guide must clearly separate health-information work from independent clinical diagnosis/treatment, privacy/legal authority, cybersecurity architecture and unsupported coding decisions.

## 1. United States — O*NET benchmark

O*NET identifies **29-9021.00 — Health Information Technologists and Medical Registrars**, updated in 2026 and classified as a Bright Outlook occupation.

The occupation applies healthcare and information-systems knowledge to computerized healthcare systems; abstracts, collects and analyzes treatment/follow-up information; may support system implementation; and may design, develop, test or implement healthcare databases.

Controlled tasks include:

- assign patients to diagnosis-related groups (DRGs) using appropriate software;
- compile medical-care and census data for statistical reports;
- identify, compile, abstract and code patient data using standard classification systems;
- plan, develop, maintain or operate health-record indexes and storage/retrieval systems;
- protect medical-record security/confidentiality;
- resolve unclear or conflicting codes/diagnoses by consulting physicians or appropriate teams;
- retrieve patient records for authorized medical personnel;
- prepare statistical/narrative/graphic reports such as tumor-registry information;
- train medical-records staff;
- support healthcare information systems and databases.

This supports the guide title directly.

Sources:
- https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/29-9021.00
- https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/29-9021.00

## 2. United States — preparation level

O*NET classifies this occupation in **Job Zone Three — Medium Preparation Needed**.

O*NET states that most occupations in this zone require vocational-school training, related work experience or an associate degree, and that one to two years of training involving on-the-job experience and informal training is typical.

This does not mean every employer accepts the same credential. Health-information coding, registry and management roles can have employer- or specialty-specific certification requirements.

- https://www.onetonline.org/skills/zone/29-9021.00

## 15. Clinical-documentation and coding boundary

Health-information workers may abstract and code information, but the guide must preserve these controls:

- code from documented clinical information and applicable standards;
- do not invent a diagnosis because it appears clinically likely;
- do not alter clinician documentation outside approved correction/amendment workflows;
- when documentation is conflicting, incomplete or unclear, query/consult the authorized clinician or coding-review process;
- distinguish coding/classification from diagnosis and treatment;
- preserve source documentation and required audit history;
- follow organization-specific coding, registry and release-of-information procedures.

O*NET explicitly supports consultation when codes or diagnoses are conflicting, missing or unclear.

## 16. Privacy, confidentiality and cybersecurity boundary

Medical records can contain highly sensitive identity, clinical, payment and family information. The guide should require:

- role-based/authorized access;
- minimum-necessary handling under applicable policy/law;
- secure authentication and MFA where supported;
- recipient verification before release;
- approved release-of-information workflows;
- secure workstations, downloads, exports and paper handling;
- prompt incident reporting;
- no shared credentials;
- no unauthorized personal cloud, USB or messaging tools;
- no protected health information in public portfolios or unapproved AI systems.

The health-information worker follows policy and applicable law; the guide must not imply that every worker independently determines privacy law or designs security architecture.

General cybersecurity reference:
- https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world

## 17. Responsible AI and automation

Potential tools include computer-assisted coding, OCR, NLP, registry abstraction support, data-quality rules and generative AI. Controlled use should require:

- approved systems and approved data classifications;
- human verification of extracted, classified, coded or summarized output;
- no invented diagnoses, clinical facts or missing values;
- source-document traceability;
- monitoring for systematic errors/bias;
- appropriate clinical/coding/privacy/security review;
- no protected data in unapproved public AI services;
- preserving required audit logs and human accountability.

NIST AI RMF is voluntary risk-management guidance; the Generative AI Profile addresses risks specific to generative AI.

Sources:
- https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework
- https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence

## 18. Accessibility boundary

Electronic health-information workflows, training materials and documents should use accessible structures and tools where applicable. The guide may teach keyboard access, meaningful form labels, readable structure, document accessibility and assistive-technology awareness, but must not claim complete legal accessibility certification based on automated checks alone.

Reference:
- https://www.section508.gov/create/

## 19. Portfolio boundary

A learner portfolio must use synthetic, de-identified-to-the-point-of-not-being-real, public or self-created data. It should not contain real patient records, real medical record numbers, real dates of service tied to real people, real diagnoses tied to identifiable people, employer screenshots with protected information, credentials or production exports.

Useful synthetic portfolio work can include:

- mock registry data dictionary;
- synthetic patient-record completeness audit;
- coding/query decision flow without real patients;
- data-quality dashboard from synthetic records;
- mock release-of-information checklist;
- EHR workflow map;
- synthetic duplicate-patient matching exercise;
- privacy/security incident-response decision tree.

## Research gate decision

The current evidence is sufficiently authoritative and occupation-specific to reconstruct Guide 81. International mappings are strong; U.S. growth and wage data are current; Canadian credential expectations are clear; Colombia has a direct CUOC match and a relevant SENA entry pathway.

**Research stage:** **PASS**.  
**Blockers:** none.
