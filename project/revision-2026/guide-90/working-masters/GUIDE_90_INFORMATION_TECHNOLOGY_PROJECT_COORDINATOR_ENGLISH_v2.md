# Lifelong Opportunity Guide 90 — Information Technology Project Coordinator

**Version:** 2.0 controlled working master  
**Language:** English  
**Primary U.S. functional benchmark:** O*NET-SOC 15-1299.09 — Information Technology Project Managers  
**Canada comparison:** NOC 21222 — Information systems specialists  
**Colombia primary comparison:** CUOC 25190 — includes Coordinador de proyecto informático  
**Colombia adjacent comparison:** CUOC 25110 — includes Analista de proyecto TI  
**Review date:** 2026-08-22

## What this career is

An Information Technology Project Coordinator helps technology work move from plan to completion. The role commonly supports schedules, milestones, meetings, actions, dependencies, documentation, status reporting, risks, issues, requirements, testing/release readiness, vendors and stakeholder communication.

The word **coordinator** matters. Some employers give coordinators substantial responsibility; others use the title for an early-career delivery-support role. A coordinator may administer project controls without owning the project budget, staffing decisions, architecture, procurement, security exceptions or production release decision.

The strongest U.S. functional benchmark is **O*NET-SOC 15-1299.09 — Information Technology Project Managers**, which is Bright Outlook and updated for 2026. Canada Job Bank directly maps *project coordinator - information technology (IT)* to **NOC 21222 — Information systems specialists**. Colombia's **CUOC 25190** explicitly includes *Coordinador de proyecto informático*; **CUOC 25110** is an adjacent systems/project-analysis family that includes *Analista de proyecto TI*.

## Coordinator versus related roles

### IT Project Coordinator
Usually supports or coordinates project controls such as plans, dates, actions, dependencies, evidence, communications, meetings and reporting within assigned authority.

### IT Project Manager
Typically has broader accountability for scope, schedule, budget, risk, stakeholders, resources and delivery decisions.

### Program Manager
Coordinates multiple related projects or workstreams toward broader outcomes.

### Scrum Master
Facilitates Scrum practices, team flow and impediment handling where Scrum is actually used. The title does not automatically own budget, product priority or all project decisions.

### Product Manager or Product Owner
Focuses on product value, priorities, requirements/backlog decisions and customer outcomes according to the organization's operating model.

Titles vary. Read the job description and ask what decisions the role can actually make.

## Why this career matters

Technology projects connect business goals with software, infrastructure, cloud, cybersecurity, data, vendors and operations. Even strong technical teams can fail when dependencies, decisions, risks, responsibilities and acceptance criteria are unclear.

A useful coordinator helps create reliable visibility without pretending that reporting alone delivers the project.

## Start with project definition

Before tracking tasks, confirm or document:

1. objective or intended outcome;
2. sponsor or accountable owner;
3. scope and exclusions;
4. major deliverables;
5. milestones and target dates;
6. dependencies and constraints;
7. budget/resource assumptions where visible to the role;
8. quality and acceptance criteria;
9. governance and decision rights;
10. security, privacy, legal, accessibility or regulatory requirements assigned to the project;
11. reporting cadence and audience;
12. definition of completion.

If these are unclear, record the ambiguity and route it to the responsible owner. Do not invent scope or approval.

## Scope control

Scope describes what is included and excluded. Useful scope controls can include:

- approved objectives;
- deliverables;
- in-scope systems and teams;
- exclusions;
- assumptions;
- constraints;
- acceptance criteria;
- approved change history.

A coordinator should not quietly add work, remove requirements or redefine completion to make progress look better.

## Schedule and milestones

A schedule should show more than dates. Track:

- work item or deliverable;
- owner;
- planned start/finish where appropriate;
- current forecast;
- predecessor/dependency;
- milestone;
- status;
- blocker;
- required approval or handoff.

A target date and a current forecast are not the same thing. If evidence shows a likely slip, report the forecast and cause rather than changing the baseline silently.

## Work breakdown, backlog and flow

Depending on the delivery model, teams may use:

- work breakdown structure (WBS);
- epics/features/stories/tasks;
- Kanban boards;
- sprint backlogs;
- milestone plans;
- stage gates;
- release trains;
- dependency maps.

The coordinator should understand the selected method without assuming that one framework is universally correct.

## RAID: risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies

Terminology varies, but a practical register can include:

- item ID;
- type;
- description;
- impact;
- likelihood for risks where used;
- owner;
- response/action;
- target/review date;
- status;
- escalation point;
- decision or dependency link.

### Risk
A possible future event that could affect objectives.

### Issue
A problem that has already occurred or is occurring.

### Assumption
A condition treated as true for planning and needing validation when material.

### Dependency
Something this project needs from another person, team, vendor, system or decision, or something another party needs from this project.

Do not keep severe risks out of the register merely to avoid uncomfortable escalation.

## Action and decision logs

A reliable action log identifies:

- action;
- owner;
- due date;
- status;
- dependency/context.

A decision log can identify:

- decision;
- date;
- decision maker/authorized forum;
- alternatives or context when useful;
- downstream impact;
- related requirement/change.

Do not record a proposal as an approved decision.

## Meetings that create value

Useful meeting discipline includes:

- clear purpose;
- necessary attendees;
- agenda or questions;
- pre-read where useful;
- time-boxing;
- decisions;
- actions with owners/dates;
- unresolved questions;
- escalations;
- concise minutes.

Meetings are not evidence of progress by themselves.

## Evidence-based status reporting

A project status report should be traceable to actual information such as:

- completed/accepted deliverables;
- milestone progress;
- schedule forecast;
- budget/cost information if assigned;
- risks and issues;
- dependencies;
- testing or quality evidence;
- approvals;
- vendor commitments;
- incidents or operational constraints;
- decisions required.

Avoid **status greenwashing**: do not hide material risks, redefine metrics retroactively or label a project healthy simply because no one wants to escalate a problem.

## Red, amber and green status

Organizations define RAG differently. Use the organization's thresholds. A defensible status model should be based on measurable conditions and explain why the status changed.

Do not assume “green” means no risks exist.

## Change control

A project change can affect:

- scope;
- requirements;
- schedule;
- cost;
- resources;
- architecture;
- security/privacy controls;
- testing;
- vendor commitments;
- deployment/release plan;
- acceptance criteria.

A useful change record can capture request, rationale, impact, options, affected baselines, decision/approval, implementation and follow-up.

The coordinator may prepare or route a change request without being the authorized approver.

## Requirements and traceability

For technology projects, requirements can come from users, contracts, architecture, security, privacy, operations, accessibility, compliance and other sources.

Where appropriate, connect:

**requirement → owner → design/work item → test/evidence → acceptance**

Do not mark a requirement complete only because development work stopped. Completion should follow the organization's acceptance process.

## Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria should be specific enough that an authorized reviewer can decide whether a deliverable meets the agreed requirement.

A coordinator can track acceptance evidence but should not declare acceptance on behalf of an owner who has not approved it.

## Quality and testing coordination

The coordinator may track:

- test plan/readiness;
- environments;
- test-data prerequisites;
- defects;
- severity/priority;
- retesting;
- performance results;
- accessibility results;
- security testing/review;
- user acceptance testing;
- exit criteria.

Passing tests is evidence, not an automatic release authorization unless the organization's process defines it that way.

## Release and production authority

Release readiness can include:

- approved scope/version;
- required testing completed;
- known defects reviewed;
- security/privacy reviews completed as required;
- deployment plan;
- rollback/forward-fix plan;
- monitoring/support readiness;
- communications;
- authorized go/no-go decision.

The coordinator can assemble evidence without having authority to deploy, waive failed controls, accept cybersecurity risk or approve production release.

## Vendor and procurement coordination

Coordinators may help with:

- requirements;
- statements of work;
- quote comparisons;
- dates;
- deliverables;
- access prerequisites;
- invoice/milestone evidence;
- vendor risks/issues;
- performance tracking.

Do not make contractual commitments, select a vendor or authorize purchases unless the role explicitly has that authority.

## Resource coordination

Track what skills, people, environments and access are needed. Useful signals include:

- assigned owner;
- capacity constraint;
- competing priority;
- unavailable specialist;
- onboarding/access delay;
- dependency on vendor or shared team.

Do not fabricate resource commitments or assign people outside governance.

## Agile, predictive and hybrid delivery

### Predictive or plan-driven
May use WBS, baselines, milestones, formal change controls and phase gates.

### Agile or iterative
May use product backlogs, sprints, Kanban flow, incremental releases and recurring feedback.

### Hybrid
Combines practices to fit governance, technical risk and organizational needs.

A coordinator should support the chosen operating model. “Agile” does not mean no planning, documentation, controls or accountability.

## Technical literacy

A coordinator does not need to be the deepest engineer, but should understand enough to ask good questions about:

- applications and APIs;
- infrastructure/networking;
- cloud services;
- databases/data migration;
- identity/access;
- cybersecurity;
- environments;
- testing;
- CI/CD;
- monitoring;
- change/release management;
- integrations and dependencies.

Technical literacy helps identify impossible dates, hidden dependencies and ambiguous ownership without pretending to be the technical approver.

## Cybersecurity coordination

Projects can create cyber risk through new systems, integrations, vendors, accounts, data flows and changes.

Useful coordination controls include:

- identify required security owner/reviewer;
- track threat/risk/security review milestones where required;
- track access approvals;
- track vulnerability/testing findings to authorized owners;
- protect security evidence;
- track remediation/exception decisions;
- include rollback and incident readiness where appropriate.

Do not perform intrusive testing or approve a security exception unless explicitly authorized.

## Privacy and data handling

Project tools can contain confidential information. Follow organizational rules for:

- data classification;
- minimum necessary sharing;
- approved repositories;
- access control;
- retention/deletion;
- meeting recordings;
- screenshots/logs;
- customer or employee data;
- contracts and commercial information.

Do not paste protected project content into public collaboration or AI tools simply for convenience.

## Accessibility coordination

Where a system or service has accessibility requirements, include them in requirements, design, testing and acceptance planning early.

Accessibility evidence may include keyboard testing, semantic structure, labels, focus, contrast, error handling, reflow and assistive-technology testing depending on the product.

Automated scanning detects only part of the problem space and does not by itself prove legal compliance.

## Responsible AI

Policy-approved AI can assist with low-risk work such as:

- agenda drafts;
- meeting-summary drafts;
- action-list formatting;
- risk brainstorming;
- status-report wording;
- synthetic portfolio scenarios;
- template generation;
- explanation of non-sensitive terminology.

Controls:

- do not submit confidential project plans, source code, customer data, credentials, contracts, private incident data or unreleased information to unapproved AI tools;
- validate dates, names, owners, decisions, requirements and risks against authoritative records;
- do not let AI invent approvals, commitments, meeting decisions or project health;
- do not let AI approve changes, releases, budgets or security/privacy exceptions;
- human accountability remains with assigned staff.

NIST AI RMF and the Generative AI Profile are voluntary risk-management guidance, not project approval authority.

## Ethics and professional boundaries

Do not:

- fabricate progress;
- hide critical risks/issues;
- change baselines silently;
- record a proposal as a decision;
- mark work accepted without authorized acceptance;
- make commitments outside delegated authority;
- disclose confidential information improperly;
- bypass security/privacy/change controls;
- manipulate metrics so a troubled project appears healthy;
- claim a certification, funding award or project outcome that has not occurred.

## Canada

Job Bank directly maps **project coordinator - information technology (IT)** to **NOC 21222 — Information systems specialists**.

### Current national wages
- low **C$28.85/hour**;
- median **C$46.15/hour**;
- high **C$68.68/hour**.

### Outlook
National labour demand and supply for NOC 21222 are expected to be broadly in line over 2024–2033. Three-year prospects vary by province or territory.

### Typical requirements
Job Bank currently says the NOC typically requires a bachelor's degree in computer science, computer systems engineering, software engineering, business administration or related discipline, **or** a college computer-science program. Programming experience is usually required, and some employers may require related IT certification.

These are occupational-group requirements, not an absolute requirement for every coordinator vacancy.

### Regulation caveat
Some professional designations associated with NOC 21222 are regulated in particular provinces, including Information Systems Professionals (certified) in Alberta and Information Technology Professional in Saskatchewan. This does **not** mean every IT Project Coordinator position in Canada is regulated. Verify exact title, duties and province.

## Colombia

## Safe portfolio projects

Use public or synthetic scenarios. A portfolio can include:

1. project charter;
2. stakeholder map;
3. milestone plan;
4. RAID log;
5. action/decision log;
6. change request;
7. weekly status report;
8. requirements/acceptance traceability sample;
9. release-readiness checklist;
10. lessons-learned summary.

Never publish employer roadmaps, contracts, credentials, architecture diagrams, customer data, incident details or confidential project evidence.

## Four-week starter plan

### Week 1 — definition and planning
Create a synthetic project charter with objective, scope, deliverables, assumptions, constraints and stakeholders.

### Week 2 — schedule and RAID
Build a milestone/dependency plan plus risk, issue, assumption and dependency register with owners and dates.

### Week 3 — delivery controls
Create meeting notes, action/decision log, change request and evidence-based status report.

### Week 4 — technical delivery and portfolio
Add requirements/acceptance traceability, security/privacy/accessibility checkpoints, release-readiness evidence and a concise README explaining what you learned.

## Questions before accepting a role

Consider asking:

- What decisions can the coordinator make independently?
- Who owns scope, budget and schedule baselines?
- What delivery methodology is used?
- What tools are authoritative for status and decisions?
- Who owns requirements and acceptance?
- How are risks/issues escalated?
- Who approves changes and production releases?
- What security/privacy/accessibility reviews are required?
- What vendor/procurement responsibilities belong to the role?
- How many projects/workstreams are typical?
- Is after-hours release support expected?
- What distinguishes coordinator from project manager in this organization?
- Is certification support available?
