# Guide 46 — Current Source Evidence 02

**Guide:** 46 — Environmental Field Technician  
**Branch:** `revision/guide-00-100-2026`  
**Research date:** August 18, 2026  
**Stage:** Current-source Research  
**Result:** PASS

## Research purpose

This intake establishes the current authoritative evidence base for the controlled Version 2 reconstruction of Guide 46. `Environmental Field Technician` is a practical reader-facing title, not a single universally regulated occupation. The controlled guide must therefore distinguish occupational mapping from job titles, supervised field-support work from regulated or qualified work, and general career preparation from site-specific environmental sampling or compliance procedures.

No legacy wage, classification, credential, training, safety, sampling, or translation claim is accepted without current verification.

## United States — occupational anchor, duties, education, wages, and outlook

### U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook profile **Environmental Science and Protection Technicians, Including Health (SOC 19-4042)** is the primary U.S. occupational anchor for the Guide 46 reader-facing title.

BLS reports:

- May 2024 median pay: **USD $49,490 per year / $23.79 per hour**.
- Lowest 10 percent: **below $36,130**.
- Highest 10 percent: **above $85,630**.
- Employment: **40,400 in 2024**, projected to **42,100 in 2034**.
- Projected numeric change: **+1,600 jobs**.
- Projected percentage change: **+4 percent** for 2024–2034.
- About **5,600 openings per year** on average over the decade, with many expected from replacement needs.
- Typical entry education: **associate's degree**.
- Related work experience: **none**.
- Typical postemployment training in the BLS quick-facts classification: **none**.

BLS also states that educational requirements vary by position from a high-school diploma to a bachelor's degree. It identifies natural sciences, laboratory experience, mathematics, statistics, and computer science as useful preparation.

BLS describes work in offices, laboratories, and the field. Typical duties include collecting air, soil, water, and other samples; labeling and tracking samples to preserve integrity; setting up or maintaining monitoring equipment; analyzing or assisting with analysis; preparing tables/charts/reports; and supporting environmental or public-health investigations. BLS states that technicians often work under environmental scientists who direct the work and evaluate results.

Field conditions may include industrial sites, outdoor work, adverse weather, prolonged standing, carrying equipment, travel, protective gear, and nonstandard hours.

BLS specifically says technicians whose jobs involve hazardous waste typically need training meeting OSHA standards. It does **not** say every environmental technician needs HAZWOPER. BLS also notes that some states have specific license or registration requirements and that employer preferences may include a driver's license, CDL, OSHA-related training, or optional inspection certifications depending on the role.

Source: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/life-physical-and-social-science/environmental-science-and-protection-technicians.htm

### O*NET OnLine

O*NET OnLine identifies **19-4042.00 Environmental Science and Protection Technicians, Including Health**, updated in 2026. The occupation performs laboratory and field tests to monitor the environment and investigate pollution sources **under the direction of an environmental scientist, engineer, or other specialist** and may collect gases, soil, water, and other materials for testing.

O*NET's reported titles include `Environmental Technician`, `Water Quality Analyst`, and `Water Quality Specialist`, supporting the Guide 46 reader-facing title while preserving the fact that real employer titles vary.

O*NET tasks include collecting samples, investigating hazardous conditions or spills, recording test data, preparing reports, inspecting workplaces, measuring samples, calibrating instruments, and providing technical/program assistance. These task statements are occupational evidence; they are not permission for an entry-level reader to perform hazardous investigations, regulated inspections, sampling, instrument calibration, or compliance determinations without the employer's procedures, training, supervision, and authorization.

Source: https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/19-4042.00

## United States — adjacent occupational boundary

BLS separately classifies **Environmental Engineering Technologists and Technicians**, whose work may include collecting air, soil, groundwater, and surface-water samples, preparing and cleaning equipment, operating field sampling pumps, monitoring instruments, and inspecting facilities. These workers operate under environmental engineers and may be exposed to remediation hazards.

This adjacent occupation is useful for showing that the broad market title `environmental technician` can span science/protection and engineering-support work. Guide 46 must not merge the two BLS occupations into a single code, wage, education requirement, or legal scope.

Source: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/environmental-engineering-technicians.htm

## United States — hazardous-waste, PPE, respiratory, chemical, and confined-space boundaries

### Hazardous waste operations — conditional, not universal

OSHA 29 CFR **1910.120** applies to specified hazardous-waste operations and emergency-response situations within its scope, including certain cleanup operations at uncontrolled hazardous-waste sites where employee exposure or reasonable possibility of exposure to safety or health hazards exists.

The controlled guide must therefore say that HAZWOPER applicability depends on the employer's operation, site, assignment, exposure potential, and governing standard. A generic environmental field role does not automatically establish HAZWOPER applicability or authorize hazardous-site work.

Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.120/

### Personal protective equipment

OSHA 29 CFR **1910.132** requires the employer to assess workplace hazards, select appropriate PPE when hazards require it, communicate the selection, provide training to covered employees, and ensure required PPE is properly used. PPE is therefore a hazard-assessment and employer-program decision; the guide must not prescribe a universal PPE kit for all field assignments.

Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132

### Respiratory protection

OSHA 29 CFR **1910.134** requires a written, worksite-specific respiratory-protection program when respirators are necessary. Required elements include selection, medical evaluation, fit testing for tight-fitting respirators, use, maintenance, training, and program evaluation. The employer must identify and evaluate respiratory hazards and select appropriate protection.

Guide 46 must not tell readers to self-select a respirator, cartridge, protection factor, or fit-testing method. It must direct them to the employer's qualified respiratory-protection program and forbid entry into uncertain or potentially dangerous atmospheres outside authorization and training.

Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134

### Hazard communication

OSHA 29 CFR **1910.1200** requires covered workplaces to communicate chemical hazards through programs that include labels/warnings, safety data sheets, and employee information/training. Environmental field technicians may encounter preserved samples, site chemicals, decontamination products, or unknown substances, but the guide must not teach independent hazard classification, chemical compatibility, mixing, neutralization, spill response, or disposal decisions.

Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200

### Permit-required confined spaces

OSHA 29 CFR **1910.146** requires employers to evaluate general-industry workplaces for permit-required confined spaces and establishes defined roles, hazard evaluation, isolation/control, entry permits, monitoring, communications, and rescue provisions where entry occurs.

Environmental field work can occur near manholes, vaults, tanks, pits, sewers, or other spaces, but Guide 46 must treat confined-space work as conditional specialized work. It must never teach entry, atmospheric testing for entry authorization, ventilation, rescue, or permit completion. Readers should not enter a space merely because a sample location or instrument is inside it.

Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.146

## Environmental sampling quality and data integrity

EPA's **Field Sampling Quality Control** page, updated April 10, 2026, publishes field-sampling quality-system procedures. EPA sampling resources emphasize that project-specific sampling plans and procedures govern field collection and that quality-control practices are needed to make environmental information defensible.

EPA sample-collection guidance describes chain-of-custody forms as records used to trace creation, possession, and handling of samples from collection through analysis. EPA materials also describe field records, unique sample identification, collection date/time and location, sample conditions, transfer records, and protection of sample integrity.

These sources establish data-integrity principles, not a universal sampling recipe. Guide 46 must require readers to follow the approved project sampling plan, standard operating procedure, analytical method, laboratory instructions, preservation/holding-time requirements, chain-of-custody system, calibration/verification requirements, shipping rules, and supervisor direction applicable to the actual project.

The guide must not teach readers to invent missing readings, backdate field notes, alter sample identifiers, silently replace anomalous measurements, estimate unobserved values as though measured, change preservation, decide a sample is compliant/noncompliant, or bypass quality controls.

Sources:

- https://www.epa.gov/quality/field-sampling-quality-control
- https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely/field-sample-collection
- https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely/field-collecting-and-handling-samples

The last two EPA pages concern a specific fish/shellfish sampling program and are used only to illustrate field-record, sample-identification, approved-plan, and chain-of-custody principles. They are **not** transferred as universal sampling methods for air, soil, groundwater, wastewater, hazardous waste, or other matrices.

## United States — education funding boundary

Federal Student Aid's current 2026–27 FAFSA guidance states that submitting the FAFSA can provide access to federal grants, Federal Work-Study, and federal student loans for eligible college, career-school, or trade-school study. Completing the FAFSA is free. Eligibility and the actual aid offer depend on the student, school, program, and applicable rules.

Guide 46 may identify FAFSA as a verification route for eligible postsecondary environmental-science, environmental-health, public-health, or related programs. It must not imply that a particular short course, private certificate, HAZWOPER class, field-training provider, or employer course is federal-aid eligible.

Source: https://studentaid.gov/articles/fafsa-student-steps/

## Canada — occupation mapping, wages, requirements, and limits of equivalence

Government of Canada Job Bank maps the search title **Environmental Technician** to **Civil engineering technologists and technicians (NOC 22300)**. This is a broader civil-engineering occupational group whose description includes environmental protection and technical support to scientists, engineers, and other professionals.

This Canadian mapping is not identical to U.S. SOC 19-4042 and must not be presented as a one-to-one crosswalk.

Current national Job Bank wage evidence for Environmental Technician / NOC 22300, updated November 19, 2025, reports:

- Low: **CAD $22.00/hour**.
- Median: **CAD $33.89/hour**.
- High: **CAD $51.10/hour**.

The Job Bank summary, modified June 2, 2026, states that prospects vary across Canada. The controlled guide must not convert this into a national guarantee or imply that these values apply to every environmental field-technician title.

Job Bank requirements state that civil engineering technicians usually require a **one- or two-year college program in civil engineering technology**; technologists usually require a two- or three-year program. Certification in civil engineering technology or a related field is available through provincial associations and may be required for some positions; supervised work experience is usually required before certification. In Quebec, regulatory-body membership is required to use the title Professional Technologist.

Because the Guide 46 title may map differently depending on duties, readers must verify the actual NOC, provincial or territorial regulation, protected title, employer requirement, education program, driver's-license requirement, and site-specific safety qualification for the job they are pursuing.

Sources:

- https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/summary-occupation/17971/ca
- https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/wages-occupation/17971/ca
- https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/requirements/17971/ca

## Colombia — SENA pathways

### Técnico en Monitoreo Ambiental

SENA's Betowa catalog identifies **Monitoreo ambiental** as a **Técnico** program with a duration of **2,208 hours**. Listed competencies include environmental-protection/SST practices, solid-waste management, obtaining representative samples according to protocols and physicochemical-analysis techniques, quantitative reasoning, practical-stage outcomes, and information-tool use.

The program profile and open-offer pages show that availability is dynamic. An open-offer view can show no current programs even though the program profile remains in the catalog; employer-specific cohorts may exist separately. Guide 46 must therefore direct readers to verify current location, modality, dates, eligibility, admission process, and open seats on Betowa.

Source: https://betowa.sena.edu.co/oferta/monitoreo-ambiental?level=2&modality=P&offertype=open&programId=109895

### Técnico en Saneamiento y Salud Ambiental

SENA's Betowa catalog identifies **Saneamiento y salud ambiental** as a **Técnico**, **2,208-hour** pathway. Listed competencies include environmental protection/SST, risk-factor inspection, risk-prevention activities, water sampling under technical manuals and sector rules, and environmental/public-health-related work.

A catalog page can show employer-specific or time-limited cohorts. A program profile is education evidence, not proof of current general-public availability, employment, independent inspection authority, or legal authorization to sign environmental or public-health determinations.

## Colombia — employer safety-management boundary

Colombia's **Decreto 1072 de 2015**, in force according to SUIN-Juriscol, establishes the occupational Safety and Health Management System (SG-SST) framework. The employer/contractor must identify hazards, evaluate and control risks, assign responsibilities, provide training, prepare for emergencies, and manage contractor safety as applicable.

Guide 46 must therefore place field-safety controls within the employer/contractor's SG-SST, actual task, site, and applicable rules. A career guide cannot authorize high-risk work, select controls for a specific exposure, or replace the employer's risk assessment, induction, training, emergency plan, or qualified supervision.

Source: https://www.suin-juriscol.gov.co/viewDocument.asp?ruta=Decretos%2F30019522

## Controlled Version 2 requirements derived from research

The English master must:

1. Use `Environmental Field Technician` as the practical guide title while explicitly anchoring U.S. labor-market data to SOC 19-4042 and noting that real job titles and duties vary.
2. Keep U.S. SOC 19-4042, Canada's Job Bank/NOC 22300 mapping, and Colombia SENA pathways separate; do not present them as exact cross-border equivalents.
3. Preserve the U.S. controlled wage, employment, outlook, openings, and education values with their dates and non-guarantee boundaries.
4. Preserve Canadian low/median/high hourly wage values and state that Job Bank's mapping is to the broader NOC 22300 group.
5. Treat sampling as controlled work under an approved project plan/SOP/method, laboratory and client requirements, supervisor direction, and applicable law—not as a generic field recipe.
6. Require sample and environmental-data integrity: correct identifiers, contemporaneous records, date/time/location, chain of custody where required, calibration/verification records, documented corrections, anomaly escalation, and protection from tampering or silent alteration.
7. State explicitly that AI may assist learning, plain-language explanation, study planning, checklists, résumé drafting, interview practice, and organization of non-sensitive notes, but may not invent field observations, measurements, sample IDs, custody transfers, calibration results, locations, photographs, laboratory results, compliance conclusions, hazard classifications, or missing data.
8. Prohibit using AI to silently normalize or overwrite anomalous readings, determine that a site/sample is safe or compliant, select a sampling method, preservation chemical, holding time, analytical method, respirator, cartridge, PPE, or decontamination procedure without the approved project/employer system and qualified human control.
9. Protect client/site coordinates, access arrangements, security information, investigation details, personal information, regulated facility data, unpublished environmental results, credentials, photographs, and other sensitive project information from unauthorized AI or cloud services.
10. Present HAZWOPER as conditional. Do not state that every environmental field technician needs HAZWOPER or that completing a generic course independently authorizes hazardous-site work.
11. Present respirator use as employer-program-controlled and prohibit self-selection for unknown/uncertain atmospheres.
12. Treat confined spaces, hazardous-material response, intrusive sampling, energized equipment, excavation entry, work over/near water, traffic control, and other high-risk assignments as specialized, site-specific work requiring applicable authorization and controls.
13. Use stop-and-escalate language for unknown substances, unlabeled containers, unexpected odors/vapors/dusts, symptoms, alarms, damaged PPE, missing sampling instructions, uncertain sample identity, broken seals, chain-of-custody discrepancies, equipment/calibration problems, unexpected site conditions, severe weather, unsafe traffic, unstable surfaces/openings, unplanned confined spaces, or work outside training/authorization.
14. Present FAFSA, Canada Student Aid, SENA, and public LATAM vocational institutions as verification/funding/training routes; never guarantee aid, admission, seats, completion, certification, placement, or employment.
15. Preserve the distinction between an educational career guide and legal, environmental, laboratory, engineering, industrial-hygiene, medical, occupational-safety, regulatory, licensing, or sampling-method advice.
16. State that employment, wages, openings, education costs, funding, licensing/certification, site access, field assignment, and earnings are not guaranteed.

## Research decision

The authoritative evidence is sufficient for controlled English reconstruction of Guide 46. Any new factual claim introduced during editorial work must be traceable to this intake or to an additional documented authoritative source before English Source Freeze.

This evidence record does not claim independent human review, legal or environmental review, laboratory or engineering approval, industrial-hygiene or medical review, hazardous-waste qualification, respiratory-protection qualification, confined-space or rescue qualification, regulatory or licensing determination, sampling-method approval, accreditation, accessibility certification, guaranteed funding, employment, or earnings.
